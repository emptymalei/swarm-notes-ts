Abstract
Unmeasured confounding challenges causal inference in intensive longitudinal studies, potentially biasing treatment effect estimates. The proximal causal inference (PCI) framework offers a promising approach to identification using proxies or negative control variables in the presence of hidden confounding bias. While prior literature considers the joint effect of time-varying treatments, our work extends the framework to a time series setting to estimate the contemporaneous or lagged effect of time-varying treatments. We demonstrate that under traditional PCI assumptions, we can recover unbiased effect estimation in the presence of unmeasured confounding by leveraging the intensive longitudinal nature of time series data. Specifically, we develop identifiability conditions and show that past and future observations can be employed as natural proxies for unmeasured confounders. We further develop the bridge function required for valid PCI estimation along with asymptotic variance. Simulation studies illustrate our method’s validity and robustness to violation of certain PCI identification assumptions. We demonstrate the potential for studying socio-environmental exposure health effects by applying our method to a smartphone study of bipolar patients. Our work contributes to the growing literature on PCI and provides a powerful tool for analyzing longitudinal data, including in observational mobile health research prone to confounding bias.

Introduction
Unmeasured confounding presents a major challenge for causal inference in intensive longitudinal studies, where data are continuously collected over time. Conventional causal inference methods can yield biased estimates of treatment effects when some confounders are omitted. The proximal causal inference (PCI) framework has recently emerged as a promising solution to this problem, providing identification strategies that leverage proxy variables to adjust for hidden confounding bias (
Tchetgen et al., 2024
,
Miao et al., 2018
).
Mobile health (mHealth) studies are one of the most common examples of intensive longitudinal studies (
Potter et al., 2023
). By enabling real-time monitoring of individual participants and delivering personalized interventions, mHealth technologies have become increasingly valuable for advancing public health. These mHealth studies, often composed of multiple N-of-1 observational studies, face complex time-varying observed and unobserved confounding where confounders both affect treatment decisions and are themselves influenced by past treatments (
Neto et al., 2022
). An example is illustrated in Figure
1
. In these observational N-of-1 settings, each individual contributes a rich time series of exposures and outcomes, and there is substantial heterogeneity in trajectories and treatment responses across (
Raman et al., 2018
). Researchers are therefore interested in individualized causal effects that vary from person to person, which naturally motivates methodologies that operate directly on within-person time series in mobile health studies rather than aggregating to a single population-level effect.
Various approaches have been proposed to estimate causal effects in time series data. One of them is marginal structural models (MSMs) that leverage the inverse probability of treatment weighting and balance the data such that the effect of time-varying confounding is removed. Its limitations are that MSMs can only balance on known covariates and the number of balancing variables is limited by sample size. Another popular approach is the g-formula. For example,
Cai et al., 2024
proposed to use the g-formula with state space modeling to estimate dynamic and individualized causal effects in non-stationary time series data;
Fowler et al., 2025
used an adapted autoregressive hidden Markov model to identify the latent variable as an effect modifier for the causal effect of interest and then obtained g-formula estimators. But their performances rely on correct model specification and the untestable assumption of no unmeasured confounding. Different from MSMs and g-formula estimators, estimators based on deep learning models such as the counterfactual recurrent network (CRN) have been proposed to study dynamic causal effects in time series data. CRN learns representations that balance the distribution of the treatment and control groups, and estimates causal effects over time while handling the bias from time-varying confounders (
Bica et al., 2020
). Like the methods above, it requires sequential ignorability assumption (no unmeasured confounding and positivity) to hold. There exist methods that could account for unmeasured confounding in time series, such as difference-in-differences (DiD) and instrumental variable. However, in N-of-1 time series data, DiD could be biased when individuals’ health states change over time and thus the parallel trends assumption is violated. Standard DiD also could not handle time-varying confounding and autocorrelation. Similarly, instrumental variable methods can be biased when the instrument is weak and sample size is small.
On the other hand, PCI offers an alternative solution that relaxes the assumption of no unmeasured founding. Recent developments in PCI theory (
Tchetgen et al., 2024
;
Ying et al., 2023
) have established sufficient conditions for identification in both point treatment and time-varying treatment settings under unmeasured confounding. Semiparametric theory for proximal estimation, along with efficiency theory, have been developed for these settings (
Cui et al., 2024
). However, despite this progress, existing work for the time-varying treatment setting has primarily focused on joint causal effects over time in longitudinal settings. The contemporaneous or lagged causal effect estimation in time series of N-of-1 studies remains largely unexplored. Our paper extends the PCI framework to address this gap by targeting individual-level contemporaneous and lagged effects in intensive time series. In a motivating multi-year smartphone study of patients with bipolar disorder, prior work using state space models under the assumption of no unmeasured confounding reported a statistically significant contemporaneous effect of text messaging on negative mood for a single participant within a prespecified time window. Yet in this setting, it is plausible that time-varying latent factors such as underlying mood or symptom severity influence both engagement in phone-based communication and subsequent self-reported mood, so that unmeasured confounding may bias individualized effect estimates. Methodologically, this work makes four main contributions. First, it formalizes a proximal causal inference framework for estimating individual contemporaneous and lagged treatment effects in intensive longitudinal N-of-1 time series, extending existing PCI theory beyond joint effects over time. Second, it shows how past outcomes and future treatments, without the need of identifying additional proxy variables, can be exploited as natural proxies for time-varying unmeasured confounders and derives corresponding identification conditions via outcome confounding bridge functions. Third, it develops a class of proximal outcome regression estimators under marginal structural mean models, together with influence-function-based inference and asymptotic variance formulas. Fourth, through simulation studies calibrated to the bipolar smartphone study and an application to the Bipolar Longitudinal Study itself, it demonstrates that accounting for unmeasured confounding via proxies can attenuate previously reported text-messaging effects and materially change substantive conclusions at the individual level. Our approach thus leverages the temporal richness of intensive longitudinal mHealth data to adjust for hidden time-varying confounding and to provide more credible individualized causal effect estimates in real-world observational settings.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Notation and the estimand of the contemporaneous and lagged effect used are given in Section 2. In Section 3, we then introduce the proximal causal inference framework and the assumptions needed for identifiability. We also propose a class of proximal outcome regression (POR) estimators and derive influence functions under the class of marginal structural mean models (MSMMs). In Section 4, we evaluate the efficacy and accuracy of our estimator through simulation studies. We consider a scenario that is similar to our data application and cases when some of the identification assumptions are violated. In section 5, we apply our estimator to a multi-year smartphone observational study of bipolar patients and estimate the dynamic eﬀect of phone-based communication on mood of patients with bipolar disorder in an N-of-1 setting. We end the paper with a discussion in Section 6.

6
Discussion
This paper develops a proximal causal inference framework tailed to estimate the individual contemporaneous and lagged treatment effects in intensive time series and N-of-1 observational studies, where temporal ordering creates a natural source of proxy information through past outcomes and future treatments. A key contribution of this work is that it leverages variables that are already observed in longitudinal data streams, rather than requiring investigators to collect additional proxy variables by design. By doing so, the framework makes proximal adjustment more practical for mobile health and other person-level time series settings in which unmeasured confounding is a realistic concern. Our simulation study further shows how the method behaves under violations of identification assumptions that are unexplored in standard PCI settings, and highlights the value of incorporating lagged history when the data exhibit temporal dependence. In the Bipolar Longitudinal Study, we find that previously identified significant results were lost once incorporating the PCI framework to adjust for the unmeasured confounders.
An important strength of our PCI framework is that it converts the temporal structure of intensive longitudinal data into a source of identification, using past outcomes and future treatments as proxies for latent confounding. At the same time, the method inherits the usual limitations of proximal causal inference: completeness, proxy independence, and bridge-function assumptions are not directly testable, and the finite-sample behavior can be sensitive when these assumptions are only approximately satisfied. In addition, our estimation strategy relies on temporal regularity conditions such as stationarity or piecewise stable effects, which may not hold in every application. These limitations warrant careful scientific justification of the hypothesized time series structure.
In practice, we recommend starting with small values of
k
k
(the temporal distance between proxies and the outcome and exposure) and
j
j
(the number of lags conditioned in the PCI framework), such as one or two lags, and then assessing sensitivity to larger values. Smaller
k
k
places the proxy variables closer to the causal contrast of interest, which is often beneficial when temporal dependence is strong. Increasing
j
j
can improve bias correction by incorporating richer recent history, but overly large
j
j
may inflate variance, worsen numerical stability, and reduce the effective sample information. A reasonable strategy is therefore to select the smallest lag structure that is scientifically reasonable and then verify that conclusions are stable under nearby choices.
While we illustrate the approach focusing on the contemporaneous treatment effect, results directly extend to the estimation of lagged treatment effects. An important future direction is to develop doubly robust estimators for contemporaneous effect as well as joint time-varying effects. Our estimator relies on correct specification of outcome confounding bridge functions. Doubly robust estimators can remain unbiased in large samples provided at least one confounding bridge function model is correct. Thus, it is important to develop alternative methods using treatment confounding bridge function as well (
Tchetgen et al., 2024
,
Ying et al., 2023
).
