Abstract
Machine learning with limited data is susceptible to overfitting, especially with modern deep neural networks. Prior to the introduction of AlexNet, reconstruction-based pretraining with auto-encoder and denoising auto-encoder were found to be beneficial, partially due to the limited size of the training sets at the time. Motivated by these traditional approaches, we propose DiffPT, a simple diffusion modeling approach to pretrain a given classifier backbone for limited domain-specific training data. We experiment on time-series data, standard natural-image benchmarks, and medical imaging datasets, and contrast the diffusion approach to a multitude of modern and traditional un/self-supervised methods. While contrastive pretraining performs better on CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet in low-data regimes, DiffPT is particularly effective on medical imaging and time-series tasks, where it outperforms self-supervised baselines across a range of low-data regimes. Our results suggest that DiffPT is a simple and effective approach for data-efficient classifier pretraining on different modalities.

Introduction
There are a variety of domains where deep learning -based classification is of interest, but the development of these models is hindered by the scarcity of data. One such domain is medical and health data, where, e.g., access to data is restricted by legislation, the modality of interest is expensive to acquire, or the medical end-point (disease, complication) is simply rare in the population. In classification, the loss asks the neural network to make only a single categorical decision per data point. However, high dimensional data, e.g., medical imaging and physiological signals, contain substantial amount of information. When labelled data are scarce, many representations will fit the observed labels equally well, and the classification objective alone provides little guidance towards the one that generalises. Training a classifier from scratch can therefore easily overfit the data.
Unsupervised pretraining used to be common practice for deep neural networks prior to the introduction of AlexNet
(
Krizhevsky et al., 2012
)
. Indeed, layer-wise greedy pretraining using deep belief networks
(
Hinton et al., 2006
)
or stacked auto-encoders
(
Bengio et al., 2006
)
was found to be crucial in order to obtain high performance with deep neural networks, and was hypothesized to have a regularizing effect
(
Erhan et al., 2010
)
. However, access to larger training sets, along with ReLU activation and better regularization, enabled direct supervised training of these networks
(
Krizhevsky et al., 2012
)
.
Transfer learning based on supervised image classifiers pretrained on the ImageNet
(
Deng et al., 2009
)
were found to improve generalization when the fine-tuning domain had only a small amount of data available
(
Raghu et al., 2019
;
Kornblith et al., 2019
;
He et al., 2019
)
. However, the utility of ImageNet transfer learning has been observed to diminish when the fine-tuning modality is dissimilar to natural images, e.g., in the case of chest X-ray imaging
(
Raghu et al., 2019
)
. As such, supervised pretraining should be performed on datasets that resemble the modality of the downstream application. Furthermore, it is highly dependent on large labelled datasets, which limits its applicability for modalities with small amounts of data.
Another line of work focuses on self-supervised learning (SSL), where the model is first pretrained on unlabelled datasets, and then fine-tuned on smaller sets of labelled data
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Grill et al., 2020
;
Caron et al., 2021
)
. SSL derives supervisory signals directly from the input data, allowing the representations to be learned without requiring additional manual annotations. However, for effective pretraining, the SSL methods are performed on extremely large unlabelled datasets. In addition, many successful SSL methods rely on carefully designed augmentation strategies. Contrastive methods such as SimCLR
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
construct multiple augmented views of the same image and encourage their representations to agree, while self-distillation methods, such as DINO
(
Caron et al., 2021
)
, similarly depend on augmentation-induced invariances. These approaches have proved highly successful for natural images, but their effectiveness relies on extensively searching for the optimal augmentations based on the fine-tuning performance. Furthermore, these methods lack a “standardised blueprint” on their application for non-imaging modalities, e.g., physiological signals and tabular data.
In contrast, reconstruction-based SSL methods
(
Vincent et al., 2008
;
He et al., 2022
)
are often simpler to apply on various modalities, and also resemble traditional unsupervised pretraining -based models. In this work, we revisit the diffusion model
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
)
as a pretraining strategy for classifier pretraining, and propose DiffPT (
Diff
usion
P
re
T
raining). Unlike conventional denoising auto-encoders
(
Vincent et al., 2008
)
, which reconstruct images from a fixed level of corruption, diffusion models are trained across a continuum of noise levels, requiring representations that capture both coarse global structure and fine local detail. Because commonly used corruption processes, i.e., the diffusion schedules, have been found to generalize across a variety of domains, DiffPT does not require practitioners to specify domain specific augmentation strategies, unlike in the case of SimCLR and DINO.
Unlike the conventional self-supervised learning setting, which assumes access to a large unlabelled dataset, we consider settings where additional data are scarce or costly to acquire, as is common in medical imaging, scientific experiments, and specialised industrial applications. Motivated by this setting, we ask whether self-supervised pretraining can improve classification even when no additional data are available. We therefore introduce no additional unlabelled data, i.e., the self-supervised pretraining and supervised finetuning are performed on the exactly same dataset in every experiment. We benchmark DiffPT against training from scratch, SimCLR, DINO, auto-encoder, and denoising auto-encoder pretraining with matched architectures. Our contributions are as follows:
•
We propose DiffPT, a data-efficient classifier pretraining method based on diffusion models.
•
We compare DiffPT with a range of self-supervised pretraining methods on various data modalities (physiological signals, natural images, medical imaging) and degrees of data scarcity.
•
We show that DiffPT matches strong augmentation-based self-supervised learning methods on a variety of modalities while requiring less computational resources.

3
Related Works
Recent works have explored diffusion models for representation learning by taking an off-the-shelf diffusion model or modifying a part of it. For instance,
Li et al. (2023)
uses a conditional diffusion model for classification by treating it as a conditional density estimator.
Xiang et al. (2023)
interprets the diffusion models as level-conditional denoising auto-encoders. Similarly,
Yang and Wang (2023)
studies the representations learned by the diffusion model at different timesteps and proposes to select the optimal timestep for knowledge transfer through reinforcement learning.
Yang et al. (2022)
connects diffusion modeling with discriminative tasks using a different approach, training a hybrid model, with the vision transformer architecture, using the diffusion objective jointly with the classification objective.
A range of studies also explore auxiliary encoder approaches by employing an auxiliary encoder to extract representations from clean inputs to aid in the diffusion process. In particular,
Mittal et al. (2022)
and
Mittal et al. (2023)
extract the infinite-dimensonal representation induced by diffusion.
Yue et al. (2024)
learns
t
t
-specific features to make up for the reconstruction errors incurred during diffusion training.
Kim et al. (2025)
learns a latent representation of the input by building an information bottleneck.
Our method is most closely related to
Xiang et al. (2023)
, as both perform diffusion pretraining followed by model truncation and discriminative fine-tuning. However, while
Xiang et al. (2023)
derives the downstream model from standard DDPM architectures, we use the target discriminative backbone as the diffusion encoder, enabling diffusion pretraining of a task-specific architecture. Moreover, while diffusion models for generation typically rely on large-scale training data, our focus on downstream discriminative performance allows us to study diffusion pretraining in small-data regimes, which, to the best of our knowledge, have not been systematically explored.

4.2
Evaluation protocols
To simulate low data regimes, we train using a subsampled fraction
p
p
of each dataset’s training split. Importantly, the same subset is used for self-supervised pretraining and for supervised fine-tuning. Pretraining therefore does not have access to additional unlabelled data. The exact values of
p
p
differ for different datasets by sample size: for CIFAR-10, Tiny ImageNet, and EyePACS, we use 1% and 10%, for HAM, we use 10%, for WISDM 50%, and for PTB-XL, 5% and 10%. In addition, we present the full data (100%) results for every dataset and method.
Since CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet have no official validation split, a class-stratified subset is drawn first, and then 20% of it is held out as validation, also in stratified manner. PTB-XL and WISDM are subsampled in a similar way, but uniformly at random. EyePACS and HAM10000 have an official separate validation split. That split is subsampled independently by the same fraction
p
p
, also stratified. We chose to use reduced validation data in reduced data settings, so that model selection does not use more labelled data than training, which is typically the case in real low data scenarios. Test sets are always used in full for precise evaluation of the generalization. We repeat each experiment over 3 seeds, each drawing a different subset, and report the mean. This captures variability from both the choice of subset and optimisation.
With DiffPT, auto-encoder, and denoising auto-encoder, the best pretrained model is selected based on the validation loss, such that the checkpoint selection is label-free for the generative models. For SimCLR and DINO, the training was less stable and validation loss was not a reliable guide to representation quality, as contrastive losses can be only loosely related to downstream performance
(
Saunshi et al., 2022
)
, and self-distillation losses are computed against a moving teacher, making them hard to compare across checkpoints. We therefore select these models by k-NN accuracy (macro-averaged AUROC for PTB-XL) on frozen validation features.
As for the classifier fine-tuning that follows the pretraining, the best checkpoint is selected by the validation accuracy, or validation macro-averaged AUROC in the case of PTB-XL. For the time-series datasets, we predict on 50%-overlapping windows and aggregate them by taking the maximum in the case of PTB-XL, following
Strodthoff et al. (2021)
, while in the case of WISDM, we average the prediction. As the evaluation measure, we use accuracy for CIFAR-10, Tiny ImageNet, and WISDM, AUROC for EyePACS, and macro-averaged AUROC for HAM and PTB-XL.
4.3
Implementation details
For CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet we use ResNet-18
(
He et al., 2016
)
, and for EyePACS and HAM10000 we use ResNet-34. We use the CIFAR-style stem in these models, i.e., kernel size of
3

, stride 1, and no max-pool, which prevents reducing spatial resolutions too rapidly that can degrade performance on the medical datasets with small lesions. As for the time-series datasets (PTB-XL and WISDM) we use a one-dimensional adaptation of the ResNet, following the implementation in
Wang et al. (2017)
.
DiffPT, SimCLR, auto-encoder and denoising auto-encoder are trained with AdamW, with a linear learning-rate warm-up followed by cosine decay, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. The classifier training in all settings follow a similar recipe. The implementation of DINO follows
Caron et al. (2021)
. Details of data augmentation are provided in Appendix
A
. However, it should be noted that all methods share the same augmentations, specific to each dataset, with the exception of SimCLR and DINO, which have pretraining specific augmentations. As the performance of SimCLR and DINO depends on constructing augmented views that preserve task-relevant discriminative signals, we conducted an augmentation hyperparameter search for these methods on the medical imaging datasets, where discriminative features can be highly localized and thus particularly sensitive to augmentation choices.

Across all our experiments, DiffPT shows strong performance, on par or better than SimCLR, which has meticulously fine-tuned augmentations for each dataset, while DiffPT follows common parametrization in the literature. Furthermore, the datasets that SimCLR has the best performance on, i.e., CIFAR-10, Tiny ImageNet, and HAM10000, contain images with the objects being classified typically covering most of the image. For such, arguably simple, classification tasks, the multi-cropping performed by SimCLR has a high likelihood of having the classification object in every crop, thus the contrastive objective directly optimizes for classification. On the other hand, in EyePACS, the biomarkers of diabetic retinopathy are highly localized, possibly just a few pixels (e.g., microaneurysms and small bleeds), and thus it is highly likely that the crops of SimCLR contain patches that indicate different classes (see Figure
1
). DiffPT does not place such location -based priors on the pretraining process, and as such has consistent performance across the range of modalities and tasks.
5.2
Computational complexity
Table
4
reports the per-step memory cost of each pretraining objective with its own augmentation pipeline. Reconstruction-based objectives are cheap: the auto-encoder, denoising auto-encoder and DiffPT need only 11–24% more memory than training a classifier from scratch, while being only approximately 10% slower. This computational advantage stems from the use of asymmetric encoder-decoder architecture, where the decoder is kept simple.
As for the contrastive and self-distillation methods, they require substantially more resources. Both SimCLR and DINO require around 100% more memory, while the throughput is 50-60% lower than the baseline classifier. The demands stem from the multi-view strategy utilised by both of these approaches: SimCLR processes two views per image (crops resized to original resolution), while DINO utilises two global (
224
×
224
224\times 224
) and four local (
96
×
96
96\times 96
) crops. Additionally, DINO requires a forward pass with the teacher network further increasing its latency, but not increasing the memory usage as much, as the teacher is not trained with backpropagation.
6
Conclusion
We have benchmarked five self-supervised learning approaches for pretraining classifiers with limited datasets. DiffPT turns out to have strong performance across various experiments ranging from time-series to medical image classification. However, SimCLR outperforms DiffPT for image classification where the discriminative signals cover most of the image area. Interestingly, pretraining with modern self-supervised approaches outperform auto-encoder approaches, with the exception of denoising auto-encoder ranking the second on limited diabetic retinopathy data. Our results suggest that if practitioners have limited classification datasets, and especially if the classification features are poorly understood, DiffPT can be used as an effective classifier pretraining method without extensive hyperparameter tuning. As diffusion models can be easily adapted to different modalities, an interesting direction for future work would be to apply DiffPT to pretrain classification models for multi-modal data.

Appendix A
Data augmentations
We use two types of data augmentation. The first is a dataset-specific base augmentation, used identically to train the from-scratch classifier baseline, during auto-encoder (AE), denoising auto-encoder (DAE) and DiffPT, and in the fine-tuning stage of every method, SimCLR and DINO included. The second is a view-generating augmentation, used only while pretraining SimCLR and DINO, because their objectives need several stochastically transformed views of each sample. All methods therefore see the same input distribution during fine-tuning, and augmentation differs between methods only where the pretraining objective itself requires it.
