Abstract
Measures of signal complexity have become increasingly popular in electroencephalography (EEG), where they are often interpreted as indicators of neural information processing, dynamical richness, or conscious state. In contrast, classical measures of temporal dependence, such as the autocorrelation function (ACF), remain more directly linked to known physiological mechanisms and are easier to interpret. The present work revisits, for the EEG practicioner, the relation between normalized Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC) and temporal autocorrelation in both synthetic and physiological EEG signals. First, synthetic time series exhibiting a broad range of temporal structure and complexity levels were generated using stochastic processes with varying degrees of temporal dependence. LZC and autocorrelation measures were then computed and compared. Across simulated signals, a robust inverse relationship was observed between normalized LZC and the first autocorrelation coefficient, indicating that signals with stronger temporal correlations exhibit lower complexity estimates. This relationship also holds in resting-state EEG recordings from healthy adult participants across channels and subjects, as well as dynamically during eyes open/close maneuvers. Overall, these results show that the information captured by LZC reflects temporal redundancy already quantified by conventional autocorrelation measures. Because autocorrelation measures relate more directly to established neurophysiological mechanisms, including oscillatory persistence, network synchronization, and temporal integration, these results suggest that interpreting complexity-based EEG findings should explicitly account for their dependence on conventional temporal statistics, which may provide a more transparent framework for mechanistic inferences.
Keywords: EEG, Lempel-Ziv complexity, autocorrelation, temporal correlation, signal complexity.

Less complex signals containing extensive repetition yield low complexity values because they can be compressed efficiently, whereas signals containing fewer repeated patterns yield higher complexity values.
Numerous studies have interpreted changes in Lempel-Ziv complexity as evidence of altered information processing, changes in neural complexity, shifts in the dynamical repertoire, or differences in conscious state

Although these findings show LZC’s sensitivity to changes in brain state, the signal properties underlying these changes remain incompletely understood
[

[

Furthermore, several authors have cautioned against equating changes in algorithmic complexity with changes in neural information processing or conscious content, noting that complexity measures often aggregate multiple statistical properties into a single index whose mechanistic meaning is not straightforward

This raises an important question: to what extent does LZC provide information beyond what simpler, more interpretable measures of temporal dependence, such as the autocorrelation function, already capture? The present study addresses this question directly.

In contrast with complexity metrics, autocorrelation analysis has a much longer tradition in signal processing and neuroscience. The autocorrelation (AC) function quantifies the similarity between a signal and time-shifted versions of itself. As such, it directly measures temporal dependence and average predictability. Time series with strong temporal persistence exhibit slowly decaying autocorrelation functions, whereas highly irregular or random signals display rapid loss of autocorrelation. In EEG research, autocorrelation has been associated with oscillatory persistence, synchronization processes, temporal integration, critical slowing, and the stability of large-scale network states.
At first glance, complexity and autocorrelation appear to represent fundamentally different concepts. Complexity is often viewed as an information-theoretic property, whereas autocorrelation is generally regarded as a statistical measure of temporal dependence. However, closer inspection suggests that the two quantities may be intimately related. The Lempel-Ziv algorithm estimates complexity by identifying repeated patterns within a sequence. Consequently, any process that increases temporal predictability should also increase pattern repetition and therefore decrease the estimated complexity. From this perspective, autocorrelation may constitute one of the principal determinants of LZC.
This note aims to illustrate and discuss this relationship systematically and is organized as follows: The next section defines the two metrics, LZC and AC. In Section III, we use synthetic time series generated from stochastic processes with differing degrees of temporal dependence to investigate the association between these two metrics. In Section IV, we use physiological EEG recordings to test the relationship we uncovered and discuss its relevance to the brain criticality hypothesis
[
23
]
. Finally, we discuss the implications of these findings for interpreting EEG complexity measures and argue that autocorrelation often provides a more direct, physiologically interpretable framework for understanding the phenomena described by LZC.

The relationship between correlations and complexity matters beyond simple time-series analysis. Previous work has shown that brain function operates near a phase transition across scales

This is not surprising, since to become ordered, the system must increase correlations. IV
Discussion
The present analyses demonstrate a robust and systematic association between normalized Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC) and temporal autocorrelation in both synthetic signals and physiological EEG recordings. Across a broad range of signal conditions, increased temporal persistence was consistently associated with lower LZC values, whereas signals characterized by reduced predictability exhibited higher complexity estimates. Although the relationship varied across signal classes, the overall pattern remained remarkably stable, suggesting that temporal dependence is a major determinant of LZC in EEG data.
From a theoretical perspective, this finding is not surprising. The Lempel-Ziv algorithm quantifies the rate at which new patterns appear within a symbolic sequence. Signals with recurrent temporal structures generate more repeated patterns and are therefore more compressible, yielding lower complexity values. Temporal autocorrelation reflects precisely this tendency for successive samples to resemble one another. Consequently, both measures reflect a common underlying property of the signal: temporal predictability. In this sense, temporal autocorrelation may be viewed as one of the principal factors governing the compressibility from which LZC is derived.
Importantly, our results do not imply that LZC and autocorrelation are mathematically equivalent. Higher-order correlations are not represented by a single autocorrelation coefficient, and signals may differ in other aspects of temporal organization. Nevertheless, the observed relationship indicates that a substantial proportion of the variability commonly attributed to the more often used complexity measures can be explained by relatively simple temporal dependencies.
This observation has implications for the physiological interpretation of EEG complexity. Changes in autocorrelation often relate directly to known neurophysiological mechanisms. Increased temporal persistence may arise from stronger rhythmic activity, greater oscillatory stability, enhanced synchronization among neuronal populations, or slower recovery of cortical networks following spontaneous fluctuations. Such mechanisms are readily interpretable within established frameworks of systems neuroscience and electrophysiology.
In contrast, changes in LZC are frequently interpreted in terms of broad concepts such as neural complexity, information richness, dynamical repertoire, or conscious content. While these interpretations may be useful at a phenomenological level, they are inherently indirect because the algorithm itself does not quantify any of these physiological processes. Rather, it quantifies the compressibility of a symbolic sequence. Consequently, similar changes in LZC may arise from very different underlying mechanisms, including alterations in oscillatory activity, spectral composition, synchronization, signal-to-noise ratio, or temporal dependence. As a result, the physiological meaning of a given complexity change is often ambiguous.
The practical implications are equally relevant. Estimating autocorrelation is computationally trivial, requires no symbolization or thresholding procedures, and offers a direct, intuitive interpretation as a measure of temporal memory. By contrast, computing LZC requires signal discretization, depends on parameter choices such as sequence length and symbolization strategy, and yields an index whose physiological interpretation is far less transparent. If both measures are found to track the same underlying property of the EEG, the additional complexity of the LZC framework may not always be justified.
This does not diminish the potential usefulness of LZC. Complexity metrics remain valuable as compact descriptors of signal organization, and under certain conditions they may capture aspects of temporal structure not reflected in conventional linear statistics. However, before attributing physiological significance to changes in Lempel-Ziv complexity, it is important to establish whether simpler signal properties can explain these changes. In this regard, temporal autocorrelation should be considered a natural reference measure against which a potential added value of complexity metrics can be evaluated.
In terms of interpretation and mechanisms, we noted the established observation that temporal correlation universally increases as a complex system loses stability near a transition point
[
23
,
25
,
26
,
24
]
. Because the critical state also predicts a set of testable dynamical properties, this feature may be useful when the objective is to mechanistically interpret spontaneous or induced (drug, arousal state, etc.) fluctuations of AC1 (and LZC) in a given neurophysiological setting.
Taken together, the present results suggest that, as expected from its construction, Lempel-Ziv complexity reflects the signal’s temporal correlations. Consequently, its use for typical EEG recordings may not capture more information than standard correlation approaches. Autocorrelation provides this information through a simpler computational procedure and with a more direct physiological interpretation. These results therefore suggest that studies using complexity metrics should routinely assess their relationship with autocorrelation and other basic descriptors of temporal structure before assigning additional functional significance to observed changes in EEG complexity.
