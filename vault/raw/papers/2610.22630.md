Abstract
Traffic agencies need current and near-future speeds on every link of their network, but their fixed sensors cover
only part of it. We study joint nowcasting and next-hour forecasting of hourly speeds on all 4,127 directional
freeway links of the Dallas–Fort Worth region from sensors on 643 links (15.6%). Historical probe-vehicle data
supervise every link in training; only sensor readings are used at inference. We call this setting
probe-supervised sparse sensing
. We propose GCRN+, a graph convolutional recurrent network that predicts
deviations from a historical speed profile. It combines a parameter-free, direction-aware kernel interpolation of
sensor anomalies, used as input features and as an output baseline, with a diffusion-convolutional GRU on the
directed road graph, a low-rank adaptive graph and learnable link embeddings. It scales linearly with network size
and trains on a CPU. Sensor readings are emulated from probe speeds, which isolates spatio-temporal inference from
detector calibration. On four held-out test days, GCRN+ attains an RMSE of 2.21 mph for the current hour and
2.39 mph for the next hour on unsensored links, against 3.23 mph for the historical average. The best baselines
that use the same interpolation features reach 2.53 and 2.75 mph. Adapted recent models without these features
(STID, STAEformer, ImputeFormer, MoGERNN) reach at best 2.54 and 2.53 mph; this comparison is between complete
pipelines rather than architectures. GCRN+ is best in every distance band except links not connected to any sensor
through the road graph. Ablations within one recurrent backbone attribute most of the gain to the link
embeddings and the adaptive graph, which can be trained because probe labels exist for every link. For congestion
detection on unsensored links, recall rises from 77% (historical average) and 86% (best competitor) to 89% at a
precision of 0.94.

traffic speed estimation; spatio-temporal kriging; traffic forecasting; graph neural

Introduction
Regional traffic operations need speed information on the whole network, but fixed sensors cover only part of it.
In the Dallas–Fort Worth (DFW) region studied here, side-fire radar detectors sit on 15.6% of the 4,127
directional freeway links. The remaining 84% of links, including entire tollways and suburban interstates, have no
sensor. Commercial probe data cover every link, but agencies often license them in historical batches for planning
rather than as a real-time feed for operations. This raises a practical question:
given only the agency’s own
sparse sensors in real time, how well can the current and next-hour speed of every link be estimated if historical
probe data are available to train the estimator?
The question combines two problems that are usually studied separately.

325 sensors), where the “unobserved” nodes are held-out sensors, typically 20–50% of a dense loop network, and
have no labels at any time. The operational problem differs in two ways. First, real coverage is sparser and
spatially clustered: many links lie more than 10 mi from the nearest sensor, and some are not connected to any
sensor through the road graph. Second, historical probe data provide labels for every link during training. The
problem is therefore transductive with respect to links, since every target link is known and labelled during

This paper studies the problem on the full DFW freeway network. Its contributions are as follows.
1.
Problem setting and evaluation protocol.
We formulate
probe-supervised sparse sensing
:
joint nowcasting and one-step forecasting on all links from sparse sensors, with probe data as training labels.
The protocol covers 4,127 links with 643 sensor links, uses day-wise train/validation/test splits, and
stratifies errors by network distance to the nearest sensor, time of day and congestion.
2.
GCRN+.
We propose an interpolation-augmented graph convolutional recurrent network. It combines
(i) an anomaly decomposition around the historical profile, (ii) a parameter-free, direction-aware masked kernel
interpolation that supplies features and an output baseline, (iii) a diffusion-convolutional GRU on the directed
road graph, (iv) a low-rank adaptive graph and (v) link embeddings. None of these components is new on its own;
the contribution is their combination for a large, sparsely observed and probe-supervised network. The cost grows
linearly with the number of links, and the model trains on a CPU.
3.
Comparison under a common protocol.
We compare the complete GCRN+ pipeline with 13 alternatives:
non-learned baselines, tabular learners, the GCRN/DCRNN/AGCRN family, the kriging model IGNNK and four recent
methods, namely STID (2022), STAEformer (2023), ImputeFormer (2024) and MoGERNN (2025), the last designed for
forecasting at unobserved locations. Only some baselines receive the interpolation features of GCRN+, so we
separate comparisons with interpolation-aware baselines and ablations within one recurrent backbone from
pipeline-level comparisons with the adapted recent models. We report results over three random seeds and a
sensor-density analysis, and we document a training failure of STAEformer in this sparse setting together with a
simple remedy.
4.
Operational products.
We test whether the accuracy gains carry over to congestion detection on
unsensored links and to a network-wide travel time index.
Because the detector archive and the probe data come from different years, sensor readings are emulated by the
probe speeds of the sensor links. The experiments therefore measure how much of the network can be recovered from
the information available at sensor locations. They do not measure the accuracy of a deployed system that uses
real detector data; we discuss this difference in Section
8

Positioning.
Most kriging and unobserved-location forecasting studies assume that unobserved nodes have
no labels at any
time
, and they evaluate on dense loop networks with a few hundred sensors. Our setting differs on both counts:
historical probe labels exist for every link, and the network has 4,127 links with sparse, clustered coverage. It
is easier than inductive kriging in the first respect and harder in the second. Comparisons with inductive methods
in this paper are therefore comparisons of adapted models under our protocol, not re-evaluations of those methods
on the tasks they were designed for.
The labels make transductive components, such as link embeddings and adaptive graphs, trainable for every link.
The network size makes dense
N
×
N

operations in every recurrent step expensive on a CPU. GCRN+ is designed
around both facts.

Problem Setting and Data
3.1
Study network and data sources
The study area is the freeway and tollway system of the Dallas–Fort Worth (DFW) region, Texas. The network
consists of
N
=
4,127
N=4{,}127
directional Traffic Message Channel (TMC) links totalling 2,222 directional miles (median
link length 0.40 mi), taken from the 2024 TMC network shapefile. Each link carries its length, number of through
lanes, directional annual average daily traffic (AADT), functional class and a toll flag.
Ground truth.
Probe-vehicle speeds for all 23 weekdays of October 2024 are available for every link
(99.5% of link–hour cells observed). The records are indexed in 15-minute epochs, but an audit showed that speeds
are constant within every clock hour (all within-hour neighbours are identical); the data are hourly values
repeated four times. At a 15-minute step, three quarters of all next-step targets would equal the last input,
which would overstate forecast skill. All experiments therefore use the hourly resolution.
Fixed sensors.
The region’s side-fire radar detectors define the sensor set. The 929 detectors active
in 2022 map to 643 TMC links (15.6% of links, 19.2% of mileage); the other 3,484 links (84.4%) have no sensor.
Because the detector archive and the probe data come from different years, the reading of a sensor link is
emulated by that link’s probe speed. Emulated readings are free of measurement error and have the same definition
as the targets. The experiments thus isolate the spatio-temporal inference problem (how much of the network can be
recovered from the information available at sensor locations) from detector-to-probe calibration, which is
discussed in Section
8
.

Recent methods.
In their adapted form and without the interpolation features, three of the four 2022–2025 methods do not improve
on the older GCRN family in this setting. MoGERNN, designed
for forecasting at unobserved locations, is on par with DCRNN (2.65/2.85 vs. 2.65/2.88). Its graph experts are a

8
Discussion
Why the transductive components help here.
Recent inductive kriging research (KITS, STA-GANN, DarkFarseer, UniSTOK) aims at models that transfer to locations
never seen with labels. When an agency holds historical probe data for every link, this constraint does not apply,
and link-specific components can be trained. The ablation (Table
4
) shows that the link embedding
and the adaptive graph each contribute and that their gains add up. The distance analysis
(Table
2
) shows that the gain over GCRN is largest beyond 1 mi from a sensor, where the road graph
carries little information. The parameter-free interpolation layer contributes in a complementary way: it gives
every link an interpolated first guess and an explicit measure of sensor evidence. With these features alone,
gradient boosting matches GCRN’s current-hour accuracy. The price of transductivity is that a link added to the
network after training, for example a new tollway segment, needs its embedding to be fitted. In practice this
means retraining or a short fine-tuning on probe data for the new link; the inductive parts of the model (road
graph, interpolation) work for it in the meantime.
Why the adapted recent methods fall behind.
Part of the gap comes from the interpolation features, which these models did not receive (limitation (iii)
below). Beyond that, STAEformer, ImputeFormer and STID were designed for networks where every node has its own history. In our setting
84% of the tokens carry no current information, and attention must learn
which
few sensors matter for each
link from 15 days of data, whereas GCRN+ receives this structure explicitly through network distances. STAEformer
learns it best: once its collapse is avoided, its adaptive embeddings make it the second-best next-hour forecaster
and the best model on links that the road graph does not connect to any sensor. It is, however, weak next to
sensors (2.61 vs. 1.89 mph within 1 mi), where explicit graph propagation is precise. MoGERNN, designed for
unobserved locations, shows the opposite profile. Its graph experts fill a link only from sensor neighbours within
the aggregation radius; beyond that radius it depends on recurrent propagation through unsensored links, and its
accuracy falls to the level of DCRNN. GCRN+ uses both mechanisms: multi-scale interpolation (kernels up to 15 mi)
and the road graph near sensors, and the regional anomaly, adaptive graph and link embeddings far from them
(Table
2
). These explanations are consistent with the stratified results but were not tested
directly.
Limitations.
(i)
Emulated sensors.
Sensor readings are emulated by the probe speed of the sensor link because detector
and probe archives come from different years. Real detectors measure spot speeds that deviate from link-average
probe speeds, especially in congestion, and they have outages and noise. Before deployment, detector readings
should be mapped to probe-equivalent speeds with a per-site calibration fitted on concurrent data. Errors at and
near sensor links will then grow, and the ranking of methods may change if some methods are more sensitive to
noisy inputs than others. A simple robustness check, not done here, is to add noise and a speed-dependent bias to
the emulated readings.
(ii)
Missing baselines.
We did not compare against low-rank completion or kriging with a covariance
estimated from the historical probe data
[
32
,
1
,
2
,
23
]
. In the
probe-supervised setting such methods can learn link-specific relations to the sensors directly from the history,
which is the same information that the link embeddings and the adaptive graph exploit. They are cheap to fit and
should be part of future comparisons, together with DGAE
[
45
]
, the closest deep model for
network-wide freeway estimation from sparse sensors.
(iii)
Inputs of the competitors.
The deep competitors did not receive the interpolation features. Without
them, GCRN+ is still the most accurate model for the current hour (2.31 mph) but not for the next hour (2.55 vs. 2.53 mph for STAEformer). The next-hour margin over STAEformer should therefore be read as the margin of a model
with interpolation features over one without them. Feature-matched variants of the deep competitors are needed to
separate the effect of the architecture from that of the input design.
(iv)
Temporal resolution.
The probe data are hourly. At hourly steps a queue can spread over many links
within one step, so much of the propagation has to be captured by the spatial operators rather than by the
recurrence. At 15-minute resolution, persistence becomes a stronger predictor at sensor links, and queue movement
is resolved over several steps, which may favour recurrent graph propagation. The ranking of methods should be
re-examined with sub-hourly data.
(v)
Duration and statistical evidence.
One month of weekdays (15 training days) is short, and the test set
consists of four consecutive days. Incidents, weather and seasonal effects are under-represented, and longer
training periods may benefit the larger models (STAEformer, ImputeFormer) more than GCRN+. The paired summaries over
test hours are descriptive; formal inference would need a day- or block-level bootstrap or a Diebold–Mariano test
with autocorrelation-robust variance
[
7
]
over a longer test period. STAEformer and the intermediate ablation variants were trained with a single seed.
(vi)
Compute budget and adaptations.
All models were trained under the same 40-epoch budget on a CPU, and
several competitors required adaptations (Section
5
). A common epoch budget does not give equal
optimisation opportunity to models with very different compute requirements. The validation curves of all deep models were still decreasing slowly at the budget limit, so
with GPUs and longer schedules their errors would likely decrease. We therefore read the comparison as a statement
about this task and budget, not about the methods in general.
(vii)
Graph construction.
Connectivity is inferred from endpoint proximity and turning angle rather than
from an authoritative topology with ramps, and the sensitivity of the results to this choice was not quantified.
The 509 links that the graph does not connect to any sensor (12% of the network) may partly result from missing
interchange connections; a routable network with ramps would probably shrink this group and change the results
for these links.
(viii)
Point estimates.
GCRN+ produces deterministic predictions. Probabilistic extensions in the style of
STGNP or diffusion models
[
11
,
14
]
would be valuable for risk-aware operations.
Deployment.
GCRN+ produces a network-wide estimate in about 0.1 s per hour of data on a CPU (Table
8
). At each
hour, an operational pipeline would read the last three hours of detector data, calibrate them, run the model and
publish the current and next-hour speed for every link. The congestion detection and travel time index products of
Section
7
can be computed directly from that output. Periodic retraining (e.g. monthly) with the latest
probe batch would keep the historical profile, the link embeddings and the adaptive graph up to date. Hours 0–1 of
each day, which the experiments omit, can be handled by taking the input window from the previous day. Before any
deployment, the most informative next step is an out-of-month test with real detector inputs.
9
Conclusion
We studied how well the current and next-hour speed of every freeway link in a large metropolitan network can be
estimated from sparse fixed sensors when historical probe data are available for training. On the DFW network
(4,127 links, 15.6% with a sensor, readings emulated from probe data), the proposed GCRN+ reduces the RMSE on
unsensored links by 32% (current hour) and 26% (next hour) relative to the historical profile. Under a common
protocol and compute budget it has the lowest RMSE of the 14 evaluated pipelines, which include four adapted
methods published between 2022 and 2025. Against the baselines that use the same interpolation features, the
reduction is 13% at both horizons; the comparison with the adapted recent models is not feature-matched. Three findings may be relevant beyond this network, although we tested them on one network and one
month of data.
First,
the availability of labels on unsensored links changes which designs work best
. Link embeddings and
an adaptive graph, which inductive kriging methods avoid, gave the largest gains when probe data supervise every
link. Second,
explicit spatial priors remain useful
. A parameter-free, direction-aware interpolation of
sensor anomalies is a strong baseline and a useful input for learned models. Attention-based models had to learn
the same structure from data, and STAEformer needed changes to its normalisation and initialisation to train at
all when 84% of the tokens carry no observation. Third,
link-level accuracy carried over to operational
products
. GCRN+ detected about 670 more congested link–hours on unsensored links over four test days than GCRN
and gave the most accurate next-hour network travel time index, at an inference cost of a fraction of a second per
hour on a CPU.
Future work includes calibrating real detector readings to probe-equivalent speeds, feature-matched variants of the
deep baselines, comparisons with DGAE and with low-rank and covariance-based estimators fitted on the probe history,
15-minute data, longer test periods with incidents and weather, probabilistic outputs, and transfer to arterial
networks.
