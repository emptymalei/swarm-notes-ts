Abstract
We present a two-stage AI-enabled framework for enhancing operator situational awareness during compressed hydrogen bunkering in maritime confined spaces. In the event of a leak, the framework first estimates the unknown leakage rate from early sensor readings and then forecasts how the hydrogen cloud will disperse over the next time steps, providing operators with a predicted concentration map together with confidence bounds. The framework is based on two independent AI models building one AI system. The AI system is designed as a decision-support tool. It augments sensor readings, and thus enhances operator situational awareness and supports decision-making. This enables preventive safety barriers to be deployed before hazardous concentrations are reached, while an independent emergency shutdown system remains the ultimate safety layer. The system design incorporates safeguards to ensure the AI system operates within its competence envelope. Trained and tested on an open dataset of confined-space hydrogen releases, the framework achieves a mean absolute error of

These results suggest that the framework may provide early information about hazardous concentration build-up, potentially supporting preventive actions by operators.

Introduction
1.1
Motivation and Background
Green hydrogen is a potential candidate for decarbonizing maritime transport
[
1
,
35
,
26
,
37
]
. Its gravimetric energy density is high and its combustion produces only water vapor. Yet the bunkering process, transferring fuel from a shore facility to a vessel’s onboard tank, introduces significant safety challenges. This work focuses on shore-to-ship compressed
H
2
H_{2}
bunkering, the recommended configuration for this fuel type
[
4
]
.
The physical properties of hydrogen increase the risk of leaks. It has the smallest molecule, enabling permeation through seals and embrittlement of steel. It is flammable over a wide range (4–75% in air) and ignites at very low energy. It is colorless, odorless, and buoyant, making leaks hard to detect and manage in enclosed spaces such as the tank connection space within a ship
[
4
,
23
]
. Currently, no dedicated maritime standard governs the system design of
H
2
H_{2}
bunkering installations. Preventive and mitigation measures are therefore drawn from guidelines
[
4
]
. These address design of high-pressure piping, open-deck station placement for natural ventilation, and dry break-away couplings.
The ultimate safety layer is the emergency shutdown (ESD), which triggers automatically when the lower flammability limit (LFL) is reached. An ESD is costly, operationally disruptive, and represents the last barrier before a potential catastrophic event. An early-warning system that forecasts hazardous concentration build-up before the LFL is reached would allow operators to trigger preventive safety barriers and thereby avoid the ESD.
Existing hydrogen sensors display only current concentration. They offer no predictive capability. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) can model dispersion with high physical fidelity, but a single simulation requires hours of computation and expert configuration to set up the geometry, mesh, and boundary conditions. In a bunkering operation, where a leak evolves over seconds and operator decisions must be made within minutes, CFD is inherently incompatible with real-time response. AI systems can learn the underlying dispersion patterns from observational data and then generate forecasts within seconds, though at the cost of precision. This computational speed makes them suitable for real-time operation. The model can estimate the leakage rate and compute the predicted concentration field. This gives operators a forward-looking view of the hazard rather than a retrospective snapshot. However, their integration into safety-critical bunkering systems must be cautious. The AI system should augment sensor readings, and thus enhance operator situational awareness and support preventive action without undermining the intrinsic safety guaranteed by the independent emergency shutdown system.
1.2
Related Work
The preceding review is based on a brief, targeted survey of the literature rather than a systematic analysis. We used
Google Scholar
as the primary search tool to identify relevant publications at the intersection of artificial intelligence, machine learning, hydrogen dispersion, and hydrogen bunkering, and subsequently verified the quality and venue of the selected works through
Web of Science
. This approach ensures coverage of the most relevant and recent contributions, but it does not claim exhaustive completeness.
1.2.1
AI systems for hydrogen dispersion
Existing AI-enabled hydrogen dispersion research focuses mainly on land-based refueling stations in open environments
[
32
,
31
,
14
]
and relies mostly on synthetic CFD data.
The first model employs a vector-quantized variational autoencoder architecture to reconstruct the concentration field of a hydrogen leak
[
31
]
. This approach differs from the required task of forecasting an evolving hydrogen dispersion and its concentration field, as its objective is reconstruction. To make decisions about triggering potential safety barriers or emergency shutdowns, the static reconstruction of concentration fields is not sufficient to support such decision during operation.
The second model functions as a CFD surrogate, using image-to-image translation to accelerate simulation times
[
14
]
. This approach reduces the computational expense compared to CFD simulations, and thus its general objective is similar to this work. However, its reliance on dense concentration-field data comparable to the training CFD grids renders its inapplicable to real-world scenarios where such data are typically unavailable due to sparse sensor networks.
The third model introduces a physics-informed deep learning framework to forecast hydrogen concentration within a fixed spatial domain
[
32
]
. However, physics-informed models require explicit initial and boundary conditions for their governing partial differential equations. An assumption that does not reflect the dynamic nature of real-world leakage events and that fails silently when conditions change.
The fourth model uses experimental data, thereby grounding the analysis in real-world physics
[
2
]
. It frames the problem as classification task, detecting the presence of a leak rather than forecasting the evolving concentration field. The study employs a Random Forest model on individual, temporally dependent samples to predict whether, after 200 seconds, the hydrogen concentration at a given location will exceed the lower flammability limit. The reported accuracy metrics are promising but given the smoothing tendencies of decision tree ensembles its generalization for unseen scenarios is questionable. The proposed approach is useful for simple alarm systems but falls short of providing a forecast of concentration levels that is needed to enhance the situational awareness of operators.
Whereas each of the mentioned studies represents a valuable addition to the study of hydrogen dispersion and how AI-enabled system can be used to improve safe and reliable operation of hydrogen bunkering, all of them have their individual limitations and fall short on three general things:
1.
They do not explicitly address compressed hydrogen bunkering in confined spaces that is important for the diffusion of hydrogen into the maritime sector.
2.
They propose methods for hydrogen dispersion but they do not discuss the implications on the system-level and how such AI systems can be incorporated into safety-related systems. This is an important stage for actual diffusion of AI systems into real-world applications.
3.
They do not address uncertainties in their model predictions. Especially during runtime, communicating uncertainties in decisions is important for human operators to make informed decisions.
1.2.2
AI systems in safety-critical systems
Before reviewing the literature, it is useful to distinguish two terms. An AI system comprises one or more machine-learning models together with the necessary supporting components, such as input processing, feature extraction, and output processing, required for the system to perform its intended function. An AI-enabled system, by contrast, is the broader engineered system into which one or more AI systems are integrated
[
3
]
. It includes conventional hardware, software, human operators, and safety mechanisms that interact with the AI components. For instance, a compressed hydrogen bunkering station can be an AI-enabled system if it contains an AI system that forecasts leakage dispersion from sensor data, but it also includes the hydrogen sensors, the bunkering control room, and an independent emergency shutdown system that closes manifold valves regardless of the AI output.
The incorporation of AI systems into safety-critical systems has accelerated with growing data accessibility, computational resources, and system complexity that may be difficult to control with traditional methods. Yet concerns about reliability and overall safety make the diffusion non-trivial. In 2024, the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) published TR 5469
[
19
]
, which is meant as recommendation for system developers and classifies AI systems in safety-related systems by usage level and technology class to determine whether existing risk reduction techniques can be applied. For instance, usage level ”D” describes the implementation of an AI system in a safety-related system such that existing risk reduction measure are sufficient and the AI system is not part of the safety function. In general, the report surveys lifecycle challenges but remains an initial guide. It offers no standard and leaves gaps without recommendations.
To improve the reliability of the system,
[
9
]
extends the ISO/IEC TR 5469 architectural patterns with a mandatory monitoring component that observes AI inputs, internal processes and outputs, and an input guard component. The monitored information is relayed to a human supervisor who decides whether to fall back to conventional control or retain the AI system in the loop. An updating component, triggered manually by the supervisor, ensures continuous model validity. Complementing these architectural views,
[
13
,
33
,
5
]
argue that assurance of AI-enabled systems must originate from a system-theoretic perspective rather than from the AI system in isolation. While these works provide valuable frameworks, they lack a concrete real-wold use case that demonstrates how these principles can be instantiated in an operational safety-related system.
1.3
Research gap and problem formulation
The literature on AI-enabled hydrogen safety-related systems exhibits two gaps. First, existing AI-based dispersion models target hydrogen refueling for vehicles rather than compressed
H
2
H_{2}
bunkering in confined spaces for the maritime sector, they rely mostly on CFD data rather than experimental data, and they do not compute uncertainties with their point estimations. No framework exists that combines real-time leakage-rate estimation with probabilistic, short-horizon forecasting of concentration fields using Gaussian processes that enable calibrated uncertainty estimation and supports operator situational awareness through sensor augmentation.
Second, the literature is divided in two fields: publications on AI systems typically stop at the model boundary, whereas publications on system design remain often abstract and lack a guidance on how AI system may be incorporated, especially in safety-related systems. An integrated treatment that delivers both a tested predictive model and a system design for its incorporation is missing.
This paper addresses both gaps. Its contributions are:
1.
A novel two-stage framework for a safety-related system for compressed
H
2
H_{2}
bunkering that estimates the unknown leakage rate via an ensemble of multi-layer perceptrons and forecasts the spatiotemporal dispersion field via Gaussian process, thereby enhancing operator situational awareness and enabling preventive safety barriers before an emergency shutdown becomes necessary.
2.
An initial system design for incorporating this AI system into a safety-related system for bunkering in compliance with ISO/IEC TR 5469, and a demonstration that the AI system can be segregated as usage level ”D” without compromising the independent emergency shutdown function.
To the best of our knowledge, no open dataset exists for hydrogen dispersion inside a ship’s tank connection space. To enable reproducible benchmarking of the AI framework, we therefore demonstrate it on an open dataset of hydrogen dispersion in a confined open-ended rectangular channel
[
15
,
11
,
16
]
. This dataset captures the key physical phenomena relevant to bunkering safety, i.e., buoyant gas accumulation in a confined volume, and a sparse sensor network for monitoring, while providing boundary conditions necessary for model validation. The framework is build so that the GP kernel structure and sensor-feature extraction can be transferred to other geometries once operational data or validated CFD become available.

2.1.2
Incorporation of AI systems into safety-critical systems
The incorporation of AI systems into safety-critical systems is not straightforward due to severe consequences of system failures. As of the writing of this work, there are no existing standards on this issue but guidelines from ISO and IEC
[
19
]
with a standard under development
ISO/IEC CD TS 22440
. This work will use the technical report and an extended view on the architectural patterns and framework for operation from
[
9
]
to guide the system design and aim to comply with the provided guidelines. For safety-critical systems, a safety-related system is needed. It consists of one or several safety functions and other risk reduction measures to maintain the safe state of the system.
The first task for the system design of an AI-enabled safety-related system is the classification of the AI system by its usage level. The usage levels are defined by six different levels from ”A1” describing an AI systems which is used in a safety system and which conducts automated decision-making of system functions to level ”D” which describes AI systems that are not part of a safety function and has no impact on the safety function due to sufficient segregation and behavior control. For instance, a conventional safety logic controller is in control of an emergency shutdown system as the safety function while an AI system independent from this provides additional information about the system’s state. After determining the usage level, the AI technology class is reviewed which basically determines if existing functional safety international standards can be used to reduce risk. Class III describes AI systems that cannot be developed and reviewed using existing techniques, class II AI systems cannot be fully developed and reviewed, while class I can be. Based on this scheme, AI systems which are classified as usage level ”D” can be implemented because existing risk reduction measures can be applied. For instance, ISO 61508 provides such risk reduction measures. This usage level also allows models for online updating which can be helpful in complex environments. Our system design incorporates the AI systems as usage level ”D” where it is part of the safety-related system by enhancing the situational awareness of operators but not part of the safety function as this is an independent emergency shutdown system based on a conventional safety logic solver. The input for the AI system and the safety function can be the same as it is the case in our system but does not necessarily be the case. For instance, in systems where the AI system is performing some kind of image segmentation conventional controller in the safety function might not be able to handle this type of unstructured data in a reliable way.
To tolerate errors without loss of reliability properties, a robust architecture around the AI system is needed. Here, the technical report suggests three different architectural patterns that can co-exist or used as alternatives to each other:
•
Backup function to the machine learning (ML) component is designed with non-AI techniques. This backup decision system is for example an alternative controller of fail-safe null action. The backup action allows the use of detection methods to switch the output when unsafe conditions are detected.
•
A safe subset of the action space is determined using a supervisor function with constraints or limits.
•
ML redundancy with output voters or aggregators is considered.

Faults in either of these components may trigger unnecessary action but do not comprise the safety of the system.

The training of AI systems is inherently probabilistic and trained AI systems are often non-deterministic or they are deterministic but due to complex environment their output seems to be non-deterministic. This makes it arguable impossible to guarantee well generalization on unseen operational conditions. In addition, AI systems are self-organized rather than human-programmed, i.e., their rules are not explicitly programmed but learned from finite data which makes it difficult to understand system behavior and identify faults. To address these challenges, the ISO/IEC TR 5469
[
19
]
gives a perspective on using the ”proven-in-use” concept from software safety standards
[
18
]
and suggests an adaption for AI systems. Proven-in-use applies only to specific products, they must be clearly restricted in functionalities, and the safety integrity level of the safety-related system that incorporates these elements remains unaffected. Evidence is drawn from documented operational evidence, specifically:
•
Conditions of use:
documented operational envelope including modes of use, configuration, and human factors that could trigger systematic faults.
•
Failure-rate history:
demonstrated dangerous failure rate below the required bound, supported by an effective reporting system.
•
Impact analysis:
analytical and test-based demonstration that differences between previous and anticipated conditions of use do not introduce dangerous systematic faults.
Proven-in-use is well defined for conventional software, but its direct adoption to AI systems remains an open question. Therefore, we draw on the general IEC 61508 principles of systematic fault avoidance and control, translated to AI training, testing, and validation, and emphasize runtime monitoring. Our discussion offers a perspective on how these functional safety principles might be extended to AI-enabled systems, rather than claiming a complete solution.
2.1.3
Safe System Design
Safety must be designed into a system, just identifying and assessing hazards is not enough to make a system safe. Therefore, addressing safety early in the design process of a system can improve the safety of the system while saving cost by avoiding expensive retro-fitting or changes in the design late in the development process. The safety in a system is not just influenced by physical hazards but also by complex interactions. The introduction of software and more recently AI systems has increased the complexity of systems. The challenge with AI systems is similar to software applications where hazards can stem from unsafe interactions of subsystems and unpredictable behavior. Thus, when we incorporate AI systems into safety-related systems the detection and handling of unsafe and unreliable system behavior must be considered. On the other hand, the incorporation of AI systems also introduces new possibilities for improving system safety and reliability in terms of increased functionality through advanced data analysis and more powerful protection mechanisms
[
22
,
21
]
.
A common technique for hazard analysis in complex systems, that can be used to design a safe system is called system-theoretic process analysis (STPA). STPA is a hazard analysis technique designed to address system complexity by using a system-theoretic approach and treating safety as a control problem. It recognizes that accidents can arise not only from component failure, but from unsafe or missing control actions within complex interactions
[
28
]
. STPA complements traditional hazard analysis techniques by capturing the increased complexity that AI systems introduce through complex interaction between technical systems, humans, and operational processes.

High variance signals that the MLPs disagree.
For the dispersion regressor, we report also RMSE and MAE on the validation set. Discussion
4.1
System design and compliance with policies
The proposed system design follows ISO/IEC TR 5469 in classifying AI system as usage level ”D” and in adopting its architectural pattern. This classification makes established risk reduction measures from functional safety standards like ISO 61508 applicable, thereby lowering the barrier for incorporation of AI systems into a safety-critical system. The proposed design must be regarded as an initial baseline rather than a final implementation. It marks the starting point for an iterative risk assessment process through which the design will be refined.
As an initial design, it is still meaningful because it embeds core safety and reliability principles. The independent ESD remains the ultimate safety layer, the AI system’s reliability is intended to be increased by architectural elements such as the input guard, and the AI monitoring. These components can be viewed as minimum requirements for any AI system that augments a safety-critical process.
For the overall system, additional conventional safeguards can be implemented. Conservative alarm thresholds in high-uncertainty regions and rejection of predictions that fall outside the system’s competence envelope, as flagged by the monitoring components.
In addition to adequate system design that promotes safe and reliable operation, human operators must also be prepared to interpret outputs from both the AI system and the independent monitoring components, i.e., input guard and AI monitoring. This preparation is essential to realize the full benefit of the AI-enabled framework without compromising the safety integrity or operational effectiveness of the independent ESD system.
This paper is not the first to draw on the “proven-in-use” approach from software safety standards. The ISO/IEC TR 5469
[
19
]
already mentions this approach but does not further elaborate. This works proposes a perspective on how proven-in-use can be translated to AI systems in safety-related systems where the training dataset represents the operational envelope, and AI monitoring and input guard ensure that the system stays within this envelope. Whereas the translation is sound, the approach needs to be evaluated in more depth.
4.2
Uncertainty-aware AI systems and human operators
Implementing uncertainty-aware AI systems does not come for free. Displaying not just point estimates but also their associated uncertainty constitutes a powerful tool when correctly implemented and, equally or even more importantly, correctly interpreted. Thus, operators should ideally participate in the development process of such AI systems from the start to develop an intuitive understanding of their functionality. Where this proves impractical, operators must receive training to interpret uncertainty estimates correctly, or an additional layer must be implemented that flags point estimates falling within high-uncertainty regions as potentially unreliable.
Operators should interpret point estimates with high uncertainty cautiously and act conservatively. They should rather trigger a safety barrier than refraining from action. In terms of safety integrity, no additional risk is introduced, and the independent ESD system remains the ultimate safety layer.
4.3
Tank connection space geometry
Even with only data from a sparse sensor network, the two-stage framework demonstrates that it can learn hydrogen dispersion physics from real-world measurements and CFD simulations and deliver calibrated uncertainty in real time. These are important capabilities required for a bunkering safety-related system, independent of the exact geometry.
The open-ended rectangular channel used in this work possesses properties transferable to a ship’s tank connection space. Leakages typically arise at the connection between the pipeline and the receiving ship. Thus, the assumption of a single, localized leakage source as employed in this work remains realistic. Furthermore, the open-ended channel reflects the natural ventilation common in such spaces. However, while a tank connection space could theoretically be designed similarly to this setup, it would more realistically incorporate active ventilation functioning as a safety barrier and contain obstructions that influence flow patterns. Consequently, the model trained in this work cannot be directly transferred to such geometries.
The aim of this work was not to build a model that generalizes to all geometries, but rather one that is easily re-trainable on new configurations. The MLP feature extraction depends on sensor topology rather than specific geometry, meaning it can be readily re-trained on new geometries. The same applies to the additive GP kernel. A complete subset kernel as in Eq.
6
can initially be trained, after which unimportant kernels can be discarded. Future work should validate the framework on different geometries to test this transferability. One specific challenge is the introduction of active safety barriers, as these represent new runtime inputs that would require a model extension to address this additional dimension.
4.4
Dataset size, distribution skew, and scalability trade-offs
The dataset comprises only 32 unique scenarios. This is modest by conventional machine-learning standards but it is representative of the operational reality in safety-critical process industries. Hazardous events are rare by design, pre-operational systems are not yet deployed at scale, and large-scale destructive experiments are expensive. Simulation data are approximations, and physical phenomena such as hydrogen dispersion remain challenging to model completely. This work therefore addresses a practical problem with extracting maximum value from small, expensive experimental data. Consequently, the framework was deliberately built to avoid large models. The additive GP structure was selected precisely because GPs offer robust generalization with small-to-medium datasets and provide predictive uncertainty, which is essential when training data cannot cover the full operational envelope. The MLP ensemble is similarly compact, and the feature engineering reduces dimensionality to match the sample size.

4.5
Model selection
The first-stage estimator was chosen for computational speed at inference, ease of implementation, and sufficient accuracy. The ensemble structure provides two additional benefits. A slight performance gain over single networks and a heuristic uncertainty estimate derived from the variance across members, which is practically useful for flagging unreliable leakage rate predictions to operators.

The second-stage model was selected because the limited dataset makes deep learning approaches prone to overfitting, whereas GPs offer robust generalization with small-to-medium-sized datasets. 4.8
Calibration
Computing uncertainties alongside point estimates can inform operator decision-making, but only when these uncertainties are properly calibrated. Otherwise, they risk misleading operators and undermining trust. In this work, the model exhibits a tendency toward over-estimation with producing prediction intervals with empirical coverage exceeding nominal targets (Table
3
). This mild over-coverage is acceptable for safety-critical dispersion forecasting, where under-confident predictions are preferable to over-confident ones. Interval sharpness remains high relative to the observed concentration range, ensuring the uncertainty estimates remain informative rather than excessively broad.
This conservative bias likely stems from two sources. First, the Beta likelihood’s learned observation noise may be inflated by high-noise regions in the training data (e.g., near the source during early transients). Second, variational GP approximations employing many inducing points can overestimate posterior variance, further widening uncertainty intervals.
Nevertheless, the model remains unbiased, and its intervals never systematically exclude the truth more frequently than the nominal coverage promises. For hydrogen safety applications, a model that acknowledges uncertainty while achieving coverage exceeding its stated confidence is far more defensible than one that is over-confident and subsequently wrong.
4.9
Two-stage framework and its hazard underestimation
A critical safety concern is the propagation of Stage 1 errors into Stage 2 especially in high-leakage-rate scenarios. The GP regressor conditions its forecast on the estimated leakage rate
m

\hat{m}
, any MLP under-prediction at high leakage rates, as seen in Figure
10

3.4
) indicate that this error propagation increases MAE by approximately 1% of hydrogen concentration. We consider this as acceptable, though improving Stage 1 reliability remains essential for ensuring Stage 2 robustness.
The system design mitigates this propagation risk through multiple safeguards. First, the input guard and AI monitoring component flag data integrity violations, out-of-distribution samples, and deviations from normal system behavior. Second, the MLP ensemble variance serves as a heuristic reliability flag where high uncertainty triggers another flag. Finally, the independent ESD system ensures that an automatic shutdown still occurs if the true concentration reaches LFL. Thus, the propagation risk is contained by the designed segregation, but it underscores why the AI system must remain advisory only.
5
Conclusion
This work presented a two-stage, uncertainty-aware framework fore real-time prediction of hydrogen dispersion during bunkering in confined spaces. The first stage employs an MLP ensemble to estimate leakage rates from features extracted during the initial ten seconds after leak onset, whereas the second stage applies an additive Gaussian process to forecast the spatiotemporal concentration field with calibrated uncertainty. Predictions are computed in seconds rather than the hours required by CFD. By augmenting current sensor readings with forecasts of future hydrogen concentrations, the framework aims to enhance operator situational awareness and promotes a shift from reactive response to proactive management of safety barriers. The GP’s uncertainty estimates allow operators to distinguish between trustworthy predictions from extrapolations beyond the training data, addressing a critical requirement for safety-critical decision support.
Methodologically, the adjusted additive kernel strategy, where a broad kernel search is followed by a refinement of the most dominant components, provides a practical heuristic for GP model selection and results in an interpretable final structure. The framework is embedded within a system design compliant with ISO/IEC TR 5469, featuring segregation from independent emergency shutdown system, input guard, and AI monitoring component.
Validation on experimental and CFD data demonstrates that the MLP ensemble estimates leakage rates with a mean absolute error of

Several limitations suggest directions for future work. The current validation reflects a single, idealized geometry with skewed leakage rate distribution. Transfer to realistic ship geometries and diverse leak scenarios requires further experimental validation and dataset expansion, potentially via active learning targeted at under-represented regions. A quantitative comparison with reduced-order CFD implementations would also be valuable for benchmarking computational efficiency and forecasting accuracy. However, such models were not accessible within the scope of this work and remain an interesting direction for future investigation. The input guard and AI monitoring component require concrete implementation and deeper investigation. In addition, the adaption of the ”proven-in-use” concept would need to be tested through extended operational validation to see whether safety assurance requirements can be satisfied. Finally, improving Stage 1 reliability is essential for deployment. Nevertheless, this framework provides a reproducible foundation for integrating interpretable, uncertainty-aware AI into safety-related systems for hydrogen bunkering.
