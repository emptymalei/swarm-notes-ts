Sample, Mix, or Verify? Allocating Test-Time
Compute under Imperfect Verifiers
Srinivasan Iyengar

Abstract
A common way to make language models and agents more accurate is to spend more compute at
inference time: generate many candidate solutions and submit the one a verifier prefers. Success
then requires that some candidate is correct and that the verifier picks it. The first grows
predictably with the number of samples; the second does not, because real verifiers (unit tests,
majority votes, LLM judges, reward models) accept wrong answers, reject right ones, and make
correlated mistakes. With a fixed budget, a practitioner must decide how many samples to draw,
which models to draw them from, and how much to spend on verification, and today these choices are
made by rules of thumb.
We introduce
SPLIT
, which makes them from a small labelled pilot. It rests on an exact formula for
the probability of submitting a correct answer under accept/reject and top-score selection that
accounts for varying task difficulty, blind spots shared across models, and correlated repeated
verifier calls. SPLIT fits the formula by empirical Bayes and uses it to plan budgets on unseen
tasks. Across seven benchmarks spanning math, science QA, code and software-engineering agents, 26
models, 175 agent systems and six kinds of real verifiers, all from public releases, SPLIT lands
within 0.02–0.8 percentage points of the best allocation in hindsight, while every fixed rule we
test loses at least 2.5 points in some setting. Regret depends only on how well SPLIT ranks
configurations, and it stays near-optimal under compute, price and latency costs and online. The
formula also explains when asking more LLM judges lowers precision. As a forecaster far beyond the
pilot, SPLIT beats power-law fitting when the verifier caps success, and we report where it loses.

Introduction
Spending more compute at inference time, by sampling many candidate solutions and keeping one, is
now a standard way to raise the accuracy of language models and agents
(
Cobbe et al., 2021
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Brown et al., 2024
;
Snell et al., 2024
)
. Coverage, the
probability that at least one of
N
N
attempts is correct, grows smoothly with
N

, often as a
power law over several orders of magnitude
(
Brown et al., 2024
;
Schaeffer et al., 2025

. But a
system is graded on the one answer it submits. That answer is chosen by a
verifier
: unit
tests, a majority vote, an LLM judge or a reward model. Real verifiers accept some wrong answers
and reject some right ones, and their mistakes are not independent. The same plausible wrong
answer tends to fool every judge, and different generators tend to fail on the same hard problems.
As a result, verified success can stall far below coverage. On MATH, majority voting over
Llama-3-8B-Instruct samples stays near 43% while coverage reaches 98% (
Figure
1
), and
Stroebl et al. (2024)
show that imperfect unit tests cap what resampling can achieve.
Practitioners therefore face an allocation problem rather than a scaling problem. With a fixed
budget, should they draw more samples, mix in a second model, spend calls on verification, or
change how strictly the verifier accepts? Fixed rules of thumb answer these questions
differently: “use the strongest model”, “scale solutions faster than verifications”
(
Singhi et al., 2025
)
, “ask more judges”. We show that each of them is badly wrong in some
realistic setting. We argue that the decision should be made from a small labelled
pilot
using a model of
selection
, not only of generation.
Three obstacles.
Fixed rules fail for three reasons, and our method addresses each.
(1)
The verifier caps what is handed in.
When a verifier sometimes accepts wrong answers,
verified success saturates at the verifier’s precision however many samples are drawn
(
Section
3
), so the verifier’s true- and false-accept rates must be modelled, not only how
often the generator is right. (2)
Tries are correlated.
Llama-3-8B-Instruct solves an average
MATH problem 27% of the time, which would give 96% coverage at 10 tries if every problem were
equally hard; its measured per-problem solve rates give 61%, because 44 of the 128 problems are
almost never solved. A second model gets stuck on the same problems (per-problem solve rates of
Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Gemma-7B correlate at 0.80), and a third of wrong MATH-500 answers fool at
least six of eight LLM judges, so asking again repeats the mistake. (3)
No fixed split works
everywhere.
The best configuration changes with the budget, the models and the verifier
(
Section
6.1
), so it has to be chosen per setting.

Related work
Repeated sampling and coverage laws.
Brown et al. (2024)
show that coverage grows
roughly as a power law in the number of samples across math, code and formal proofs, and document
that majority voting and reward-model selection plateau well below coverage.
Schaeffer et al. (2025)
explain the power law through a heavy-tailed distribution of
per-problem success rates. Both works model
coverage

the selection factor, a treatment of correlated errors, and pilot-calibrated allocation; our
coverage component uses the same beta-binomial view of difficulty.
Chen et al. (2024)
show that voting accuracy can be
non-monotone in the number of calls, depending on the mix of easy and hard queries. We obtain a
related non-monotonicity for
verification

(
Proposition
2
).
Imperfect verifiers.
Stroebl et al. (2024)
show that false positives of unit tests
bound the accuracy of resampling, that a model’s single-sample accuracy is strongly correlated with
its false-positive rate on HumanEval and MBPP, and that the best number of attempts is often small.
We build on their observations. We replicate the accuracy–FPR correlation on HumanEval+, MBPP+ and
CodeContests and refine it for scored verifiers (
Section
6.3
). We also turn the bound into
a predictive model with correlated errors, estimated from a pilot, and use it for allocation.

(

Limitations and conclusion
Limitations.
•
Point forecasts.
SPLIT’s advantage over power-law extrapolation is large only
under a simulated verifier and on SWE-bench thresholds. It is small (0.4–0.6 points) with real
tests, absent for pure coverage, and negative for argmax selection (
Table
6
). Our
claims rest on decisions and mechanisms, not on forecasting accuracy.
•
Same-policy agent horizons.
The longest same-agent horizon we can test is 16
held-out rollouts per issue, because no public release we found has more. The 175-system pool
tests long horizons for coverage only, with no shared verifier. Testing selection at long
horizons with one agent needs new rollouts. We release a pipeline that collects, scores and
analyses them (
Appendix
E
), but have not run it: at 64 rollouts per issue it needs about
32,000 agent runs.
•
Representative pilots.
All estimators assume the pilot is exchangeable with
deployment. Under capability drift (pilot of older systems), every method fails for small
pilots (
Section
6.4
). Some refinements also need labelled calibration tasks with
repeated judge verdicts.
•
Costs.
Prices and latency enter through a one-parameter family,
(
parameters
)
γ
×
(\text{parameters})^{\gamma}\times
measured tokens, not through quoted provider prices. Latency is
modelled as additive cost, which ignores parallel sampling. Judge calls are priced as one
generation.
•
Model choice.
Neither the pooled nor the task-varying verifier model is best on every
dataset. Our out-of-sample selection rule is never the worse of the two but is significantly
behind the better one on two of three datasets (
Appendix
C
).
•
Theory.
The propositions are exact under conditional independence of candidates
given the task (
Assumption
1
). Dependence among candidates
within
a task, such as
duplicated or near-duplicated answers, is not modelled and may bias SPLIT, especially at large
N
N
. The verifier propensity models repeated calls on one candidate, not correlated candidates. The
planner’s finite-sample regret guarantee (
Proposition
4
) is vacuous at our task counts; it
explains consistency, not the size of the observed regret.
•
Online policy.
The re-planning policy is a greedy index, not an optimal sequential
policy. It ignores the risk that a false acceptance ends a task, and is evaluated only on
CodeContests, by simulation from measured per-task rates.
•
Agent pool.
The 175-system experiment treats different systems as exchangeable
attempts. This tests the difficulty prior at long horizons but is not a test of one agent’s
repeated sampling or of selection.
Conclusion.
Test-time compute is limited by selection as much as by generation. A
small labelled pilot, interpreted through an exact success formula that accounts for difficulty,
shared blind spots and correlated verifier errors, is enough to decide how many samples to draw,
which generators to mix, and how much to verify. Across math, science QA, code and agents this
decision is within a fraction of a point of the best possible, where fixed rules can lose 2.5 to 20
points. The planner needs only to rank configurations, so its regret is bounded by differential,
not absolute, forecast error, and it stays near-optimal when samples are priced by compute, by
sublinear per-token prices or by latency. The same model explains when re-verification backfires and
why pooled verifier statistics mislead. Re-planning online, by stopping a task at its first
accepted candidate and moving the savings to unresolved tasks, recovers the gap to the best static
configuration in hindsight. The most useful next step is to test selection on long same-agent
rollouts, for which we release a ready-to-run pipeline.
Broader Impact Statement
This work helps practitioners spend inference compute more efficiently, which can reduce the
cost and energy use of deployed language-model systems. It also documents failure modes that
matter for oversight. Stronger generators produce wrong outputs that pass fixed checks more often,
and asking more LLM judges can make acceptance of wrong answers
more
likely when judges share
blind spots. Systems that rely on automated verification for safety-relevant decisions should
re-measure verifier error rates whenever the generator changes, and should not assume that
majority votes of judges converge to the truth. Our planner optimises measured success on a
benchmark-defined notion of correctness; when that notion is itself flawed (e.g. weak tests), the
planner will optimise the flawed target. All data are public releases; we collected no human data.
