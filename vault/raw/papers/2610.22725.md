Abstract
In the age of large automated surveys conducted at facilities like the NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory, delivering precision science goals demands highly efficient scheduling strategies that adapt to changing environments on the summit. On site, a variety of telemetry streams can be used to inform a scheduler that is incentivized to find the best next pointing given atmospheric transmission. At Rubin, live upward-facing monitoring is provided by the Dutch Rubin Enhanced Atmospheric Monitor (DREAM) all-sky camera. Currently, dynamic scheduling uses short-term predictive algorithms, such as tracking cloud cross-correlations, to anticipate movement at short (
<
10
<10
min) scheduling timescales. However, many of Rubin’s core science objectives require survey strategies to commit to observations 20–40 min in advance (pair mode), which remains out of reach because clouds at those timescales can arrive from beyond the horizon. Here, we report on a novel multimodal strategy that folds asynchronous data from both the DREAM camera and weather satellite imagery to forecast multi-horizon sky transmission at 90 s, 5 min, 20 min, and 30 min in advance. We demonstrate that at 20-minute timescales, our model reduces transmission error by 21% and false-clear misses by 22% versus Rubin’s frozen-flow tracker, and reduces false-clear misses by 26% versus persistence. We also demonstrate empirically, through ablations, that the DREAM all-sky camera anchors the short-horizon predictive power while satellite imagery provides long-horizon predictive power. We estimate the optimistic order-of-magnitude gain for LSST survey efficiency improvement to be between 0.1% and 1%.

1.4
Classical Transmission Forecasting
Forecasting, sometimes called nowcasting at short horizons, has been studied extensively. Classical models rely on the kinematics of historically observed data to forward-model trajectories of future events. Often, on short timescales, the zeroth-order solution, and sometimes a sufficient one, is to assume that nothing changes: Eulerian persistence from frame to frame
[
12
,
37
]
. It has been shown that within short timescales this can be competitive; for example, some all-sky solar imagers used the same freeze as the intra-hour reference
[
7
,
54
]
. The competitiveness of these simplest possible solutions is that standing cloud decks and slow cirrus can often change little over tens of minutes. Thus a copy can often be predictive. The clear bottleneck here is that it encodes no motion; cloud that translates, grows, or enters from off-frame is a miss
[
30
]
.
The next upgrade to persistence is to estimate the motion successively through multiple images and then advect and move the clouds. In many cases, Lucas and Kanade’s local optical flow (OF)
[
25
,
4
]
can be used as the velocity vector for rain-rate nowcasting
[
5
]
or for all-sky cloud tracking
[
7
]
. At its core, OF assumes that objects remain constant in brightness and, under that assumption, tracks pixels in time to forward-model the next step. Further advances include semi-Lagrangian backtraces
[
42
]
, a mix of the Eulerian scheme (stationary grids) and a fully Lagrangian scheme with moving grids. Semi-Lagrangian advection still uses a fixed Eulerian grid to store transmission values, but filling a mesh point follows a fluid parcel. Further methods include scale-cascade schemes such as S-PROG
[
37
]
and pysteps
[
30
]
, which keep the same Lagrangian skeleton while matching lifetime to spatial scale. However, these frozen-flow approximations cannot represent cell growth or decay, stacked layers at different speeds, or cloud that has not yet entered the frame. The velocity estimate is also noisy.
Later work combined standard optical-flow models with telemetry from physical wind measurements to reduce the noise in the gradient calculation. That kinematic line replaced the camera velocity with an independent meteorological wind. Cloud outside the camera at
t
end
t_{\mathrm{end}}
nevertheless remains invisible at long enough horizons.
The current proposed dynamic scheduler for the FBS, detailed in
[
38
]
, implements a similar camera-inferred motion model. A frame-to-frame cross-correlation under a frozen-flow assumption uses the DREAM maps to determine the next pointing. Its primary goal is to produce a clean global cloud velocity vector from successive DREAM frames, then use it to keep the telescope inside the moving clear hole for the next
∼
\sim
32 s visit. The velocity estimate dithers the transmission map in various directions and magnitudes, computes a Pearson correlation with the true successive frame, and selects the perturbation that maximizes the correlation. The optimal perturbation is used to compute the global velocity vector. This has produced large immediate gains in survey efficiency
[
38
]
.
1.5
Deep-Learning-Based Forecasting
Classical models’ reliance on optical-flow or frozen-flow assumptions is a limitation of their design. Upgrading those models to full fluid simulations remains inaccessible at these nowcast latencies. There is therefore a growing interest in data-driven solutions, with the goal of learning that residual from a window of fields, without an explicit velocity, from the observed data itself.
One of the foundational architectures in this problem domain is the convolutional long short-term memory (ConvLSTM)
[
39
]
model. Naturally, one can imagine casting this as a sequence-modeling problem. However, a standard long short-term memory (LSTM) treats each timestep as a feature vector, which destroys spatial structure and is too expensive for a map. ConvLSTM replaces those gates with convolutions so the hidden and cell states remain spatial fields. Recurrence then acts as an implicit memory of local advection. The network can learn to move, grow, or fade structure that a frozen-flow tracker can only translate. Later, recurrent models added longer temporal memory (PredRNN)
[
52
]
, trajectory-gated motion (TrajGRU)
[
40
]
, or a partial differential equation (PDE)-constrained branch beside a residual cell (PhyDNet)
[
14
]
.
A second family of models dropped this recurrence structure. Simpler video prediction (SimVP)
[
11
]
stacks the history on the channel axis and runs an encoder–translator–decoder: spatial mixing in the encoder and decoder, a residual convolutional neural network (CNN) translator at the bottleneck. The claim is that a feed-forward translator on stacked frames matches or beats recurrent video models without the cost of unrolling. Larger models then brought cuboid and axial attention (Earthformer, MetNet)
[
10
,
46
]
and generative nowcasts (DGMR, NowcastNet)
[
32
,
57
]
.
In this work, we choose to focus on the two architectural paradigms, ConvLSTM and SimVP, because other larger models require much longer data baselines, while the present work supplies only one year of training data.

1.7
Problem Statement
Given the advancements already made, the objective is clear. We ask whether a predictive model can operate across multiple long time horizons and cover blind spots in the survey strategy. We forecast summit transmission using real DREAM and GOES data at leads that the scheduler consumes: 90 s for the in-flight FBS queue, 5 min for short-horizon dynamic pointing, 20 min for a lower bound on pair-mode lookahead, and 30 min as the longest trained lead (approximately the

\sim

Model Architecture
The architecture of our model comes with a variety of design choices to optimally leverage our multimodal dataset. We first designed custom encoders for the spherical DREAM camera data, the satellite imagery, the vector point-cloud wind data, and the numerical metadata. After the encoder, we employ geometry fusion to combine features from flat geometry with spherical geometry and unite the modalities. We also developed methods of stitching the data onto the same temporal backbone by mixing features to handle multi-cadence input.

4.3
Future Deployment Pathway and Online Training Plan
Deploying a data-driven model requires maintenance, as any other software or hardware system does. That maintenance has to be regular if the data distribution drifts, especially under a changing climate. Routine training or finetuning should therefore run during the day, before observations, on recent nights. A simple scheme finetunes the model and weights recent observations more heavily than the historical DREAM archive, so the prior tracks the latest weather. The training reported here weights nights equally. In operations, recent nights are likely to be more informative than older ones.
