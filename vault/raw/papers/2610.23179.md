Abstract
Efficient irrigation management requires timely responses to soil moisture conditions and anticipated weather changes. This article presents an embedded, weather-based irrigation system that integrates soil moisture monitoring using multiple sensors, edge-based decision-making, automated pump control, and local data management. Four capacitive soil moisture sensors are connected to an STM32F103C8T6 microcontroller for data acquisition, while a BTT Pi V1.2 platform evaluates the sensor data, retrieves short-term weather forecasts, makes irrigation decisions, manages a graphical user interface, and stores operational data locally. Individual sensor readings serve as the basis for zone-level irrigation decisions, while the median of four time-averaged readings supports monitoring and analysis. Forecasted precipitation is taken into account as effective precipitation to reduce the calculated irrigation requirement before the pump is activated. The system was evaluated as part of a one-month cultivation trial with radishes, during which a total of 38,112 data points from measurement cycles were recorded. The median value of normalized soil moisture averaged 47.93% and remained predominantly within the configured control range. Three irrigation events with a planned total irrigation volume of 26.68 L were recorded. Finally, we demonstrate the technical feasibility of integrating sensors, weather data, local data processing, automated control, and data collection into a scalable platform for precision irrigation.

Introduction
Water management in agriculture is increasingly critical due to rising water demand, climatic fluctuations, and the need for sustainable food production
[
1
,
2
]
. Fixed-schedule irrigation often fails to account for dynamic conditions such as soil moisture fluctuations, precipitation events, and changing atmospheric conditions
[

]
, leading to over- or under-irrigation
[
4
]
. Smart irrigation strategies that adapt to real-time, on-site conditions are therefore essential for water-use efficiency and sustainable agriculture
[
5
]
.
Precision agriculture and IoT technologies have enabled automated irrigation based on environmental sensors, wireless communication, and embedded computer systems. Soil moisture sensors are widely used because they provide direct information about the water status of the growing medium
[
6
]
and make it possible to overcome the limitations of time-based irrigation schedules through control loops
[
7
]
. Various strategies have been investigated, including control based on moisture thresholds, evapotranspiration-based scheduling, and weather-based management
[
8
]
. Moisture-based methods directly reflect plant growth conditions but do not account for impending precipitation, while weather-based approaches can reduce unnecessary irrigation but depend on the accuracy of forecasts and a reliable computational infrastructure
[
9
]
. Combining both approaches enables more adaptive irrigation decisions.
Despite this progress, many existing solutions are based on cloud architectures that limit their use in remote areas, focus on algorithms without full embedded implementations, or lack practical, long-term validation in the field. To address these gaps, this article presents an embedded, weather-based irrigation system that combines four capacitive soil moisture sensors and an STM32F103C8T6 microcontroller for data acquisition with a BTT Pi V1.2 edge platform, which handles median-based sensor fusion, irrigation decision-making, weather data retrieval, graphical user interface operation, and database management.
The main contributions of this work are:
•
A fully integrated irrigation architecture that has been experimentally evaluated and combines multisensor soil moisture measurement, edge computing, weather forecast data, automated control, and data management.
•
A median-based sensor fusion method on the BTT Pi V1.2 for determining a representative soil moisture value from four independent capacitive sensors.
•
A weather-based decision strategy that uses short-term forecasts to postpone unnecessary irrigation before predicted precipitation.
•
A practical experimental evaluation using radish plants, which provides a realistic dataset on soil moisture, irrigation events, and weather information.

II
System Architecture
The proposed system is an embedded cyber-physical architecture that integrates environmental sensing, edge-based decision-making, automated control, and user interaction. Unlike conventional systems, which rely on fixed schedules or isolated measurements, it combines real-time soil moisture data with short-term weather forecasts. The architecture consists of three layers—sensing, processing/decision-making, and control/monitoring—as shown in Figure
1
.
Fig. 1:
Overall architecture of the proposed weather-dependent embedded irrigation system.
The data acquisition layer (four capacitive sensors and the STM32F103C8T6) forwards preprocessed measurement values to the processing layer (BTT Pi V1.2), which combines the sensor data with forecasts from the weather API to generate irrigation commands. These commands control the relay-controlled pumps and update both the database and the graphical user interface in the control/monitoring layer.
II-A
Sensor Layer
Four capacitive soil moisture sensors, distributed across the cropland to detect spatial variations, provide data to the STM32F103C8T6 for local preprocessing. Since individual sensors may vary due to soil heterogeneity, placement, and noise, a median-based fusion approach provides a representative value for monitoring
[
10
,
11
]
, while individual measurements drive zone-level control.
II-B
Edge Computing and Decision-Making Level
The BTT Pi V1.2 executes the irrigation algorithm, retrieves weather forecasts, manages data, and provides the graphical user interface (GUI). It receives the four normalized moisture values and combines them with short-term forecasts from an online weather API. If the moisture content in a zone falls below the threshold, the controller calculates the effective precipitation based on the forecast and activates irrigation only if the net demand remains positive. This decentralized design reduces the computational load on the sensor nodes and enables flexible control updates at the edge, thereby improving responsiveness
[
12
,
13
]
. Weather data is cached in memory once per interval (default: one hour); without a persistent cache or the use of simulated data, API errors can interrupt the decision-making cycle.
II-C
Execution and Monitoring Levels
The edge controller sends pump commands via a serial interface to the STM32F103C8T6, which controls the relay module and the individual pumps. In addition to automated irrigation, the system stores environmental measurements, weather data, irrigation operations, and status parameters in a local database and features a graphical user interface for visualization and configuration. Irrigation decisions are based on sensor readings taken prior to irrigation, and pump operation is time-controlled without real-time feedback during irrigation.
III
Methodology
The proposed methodology combines moisture monitoring using multiple sensors, local sensor fusion, weather forecasts, and automated pump control to plan irrigation based on actual soil conditions while avoiding unnecessary irrigation when rain is expected. The STM32F103C8T6 acquires the four measured values and sends them to the BTT Pi V1.2, which handles sensor fusion, retrieving weather data, decision-making, database management, and operation of the graphical user interface, as shown in the flowchart in Figure

IV
Implementation
The system was implemented as an embedded edge platform that integrates moisture measurement, sensor processing, weather data retrieval, decision-making, control, and logging, and was tested over a 30-day radish growing period.

The STM32 acquires and forwards the four measured values; the BTT Pi V1.2 calculates the median and performs higher-level processing steps (weather API, decision-making, database, GUI). V
Results
This section describes the experimental setup, methodology, and data analysis based on real-world field measurements, with a focus on soil moisture trends, irrigation events, prevailing weather conditions, and water application rates.
V-A
Experimental Setup
The system was evaluated in a one-month cultivation trial with radishes (
Raphanus sativus
) in a large container, with four sensors distributed across the plot and all four pumps in operation. Irrigation decisions were based on measurements from individual zones, plant information, and weather forecasts, while the median supported monitoring. The analysis period spanned from the 13th of one month to the 13th of the following month, during which all subsystems were in continuous operation.
V-B
Experimental Procedure
In each cycle, the sensor readings were recorded over a period of 60 s, and time-averaged values and the median were calculated (Section
III
), followed by a 300-s waiting period before the process was repeated. The controller combined the median moisture content, the weather forecast, and the plant parameters; if the moisture content indicated a need for irrigation, effective precipitation reduced this need, and irrigation was activated only if the net demand remained positive, with commands relayed to the pumps via the STM32. All measured values, decisions, and events were continuously logged.
V-C
Measurements and Analysis
The most important recorded variables include the time-averaged values per sensor, the median moisture content, weather forecasts, irrigation events, water application rates, and timestamps. The moisture percentages are normalized sensor values derived from a linear mapping (dry = 3000, wet = 1200) and limited to 0–100%; they represent a normalized output value and not a calibrated volumetric content, as no independent calibration was performed. Soil-moisture-based irrigation planning is well established and can reduce water consumption while maintaining yield
[
16
]
; accordingly, the median of four sensors—rather than the value from a single sensor—is used here as the primary indicator.

VI
Analysis
This section examines the statistical properties of soil moisture, its relationship to environmental variables, its behavior during irrigation events, the response of soil moisture to irrigation, and the consistency of the sensor measurements.
VI-A
Statistical Characterization of Soil Moisture
The dataset contains 38,112 aggregated cycle records from June 13 to July 13, 2026 (the sensors recorded data every 2 seconds, averaged over 60-second windows), which were generated using an older control algorithm that differs from the current version. The median soil moisture value was 47.93%, with a standard deviation of 3.84 percentage points; the values ranged from 32.2% to 54.0%. With configured thresholds of 40% (lower) and 65% (upper), only 1 out of 38,112 data points fell below the lower threshold ( 0.0026%), and none reached the upper threshold, indicating stable conditions consistent with threshold-based control. The lowest value (32.2%) triggered irrigation; the highest value (54.0%) remained below the upper limit. Gradual decreases in moisture between events, followed by increases due to irrigation or rain, suggest that the sensors captured meaningful dynamics of the water balance.

VI-C
Analysis of Irrigation Cycles
Three irrigation events were recorded, with planned storage volumes of 11.93 L, 7.37 L, and 7.38 L (for a total of 26.68 L)—these are values calculated by the controller and not measured flow rates (the database field for flow rate is set to zero for all older data records). The first irrigation cycle (June 13) took place when soil moisture was at 32.2% and there was no precipitation forecast. The second cycle (June 22) occurred when soil moisture was 40.0%, even though there was a precipitation forecast of approximately 0.8 mm (for that day) and 2.1 mm (total over two days), which shows that while weather data is taken into account, it does not override irrigation triggered by threshold values. The third incident (July 10) occurred when soil moisture was 40.0% with no rain forecast. Conversely, on June 28, a forecast of approximately 22.2 mm was not preceded by any irrigation, and the moisture content rose from 47.4% to 53.3% over the following two days, illustrating the substrate’s response to the expected precipitation.
VI-D
Soil Moisture Response to Irrigation
During the irrigation events on June 22 and July 10, the median soil moisture content rose from approximately 40.1–40.2% before irrigation to approximately 52.6% within 30 minutes after irrigation—an increase of about 12.5 percentage points in both cases. This consistency suggests a reproducible short-term relationship between irrigation and measurable changes in moisture, although the lack of pump telemetry means that the physical triggering cannot be independently verified based on the recorded data. The response should be interpreted more as a system-level indicator rather than as a measure of irrigation efficiency or plant water uptake.

VI-F
Sensor Agreement and Spatial Variability
The average moisture content per sensor ranged from 46.28% (S1) to 48.98% (S4), corresponding to a standard deviation of approximately 2.70 percentage points, which is consistent with the expected spatial variability of soil properties, water distribution, and sensor placement. The median of the four values, which is used as a representative indicator, provides a robust central estimate that is less sensitive to individual outliers than an arithmetic mean.
VI-G
Statistical Interpretation, Scope, and Future Work
Since the experiment was conducted in a single growing environment without a parallel control group and only three irrigation events were recorded, the dataset does not allow for statistically sound conclusions regarding water savings or generalizations to other cycles; however, it offers high temporal resolution for characterizing moisture, weather, and dynamics at the event level
[
17
]
. Future work should include a controlled comparison test with timed irrigation, replicates across multiple growth cycles and seasons, and calibration of the assumed pump flow rate of 1.6 L/min using a flow meter to validate the estimated flow rates.

Reliable embedded hardware that integrates sensors, computing power, communication, and actuators remains indispensable for practical applications

Overall, previous work has often focused on individual components (data collection, forecasting, or cloud data management) rather than on comprehensive, field-tested platforms. This paper fills that gap with an integrated, weather-based embedded irrigation system that was evaluated using field data from radish cultivation and combines current soil conditions with forecast information to avoid unnecessary irrigation.
VIII
Discussion
The results demonstrate the technical feasibility of integrating multisensor moisture measurement, edge computing, weather data, and automated irrigation into an embedded platform using a sensor-driven paradigm in which weather data supplements the context rather than replacing direct measurements. Using the median of four spatially distributed sensors that showed similar trends but different absolute values yields a fused result that is more robust against outliers than a simple average, while the division of labor between the STM32 and the BTT Pi decouples low-level data acquisition from high-level control.
The irrigation events coincided with low moisture levels, while a major forecasted precipitation event (June 28) was followed by an increase in moisture without triggering irrigation—which is consistent with the fact that forecasts were used as a supplementary factor rather than as the sole determining factor. In the two events that could be analyzed (June 22, July 10), the moisture content rose by about 12.5 points within 30 minutes of irrigation—a repeatable short-term signal, although the lack of telemetry data from the pumps limits an independent verification of the trigger. An important insight from the implementation is the large discrepancy between the calculated water requirements of the crops and the target values stored in the old algorithm, which illustrates that irrigation duration, pump flow rate, and application efficiency must be directly calibrated and cannot be based on assumptions. Similarly, the non-significant correlations between soil moisture and individual weather variables reflect the fact that soil moisture is a state variable influenced by many combined factors—and do not prove that the weather is unimportant.
Using local (edge) computing on the BTT Pi V1.2 instead of a remote cloud service reduces dependence on network availability—a valuable advantage for agricultural applications. The associated database and graphical user interface (Figure
5
) extend the platform beyond simple pump automation and enable historical analyses as well as operator review without the need to query the database directly.

Graphical user interface of the implemented irrigation system, displaying real-time sensor readings, weather information, and system status.
Despite the robust implementation, there are some limitations to consider. The experimental design, which was limited to a single environment and did not include a control group, does not allow for reliable conclusions regarding the percentage of water savings, yields, or water productivity. Only three irrigation events were recorded, which limits statistical generalizability despite the large number of moisture measurement cycles. In the future, we will expand this work through comprehensive data collection over the course of a year to achieve significantly more precise results. The weather-dependent component also depends on the quality of external forecasts; incorporating forecast reliability rather than a deterministic interpretation of the forecasts could improve robustness.
Notwithstanding these limitations, the results provide a practical foundation for further development. The modular combination of decentralized sensors, edge fusion, weather-aware decision-making, automated control, and continuous logging can be extended to other crops and environments, thereby supporting the overarching goal of scalable platforms for smart agriculture. Future work will expand data collection across multiple growing seasons and investigate machine learning-based predictive planning.
IX
Conclusion
This article presents the design, implementation, and experimental evaluation of an embedded, weather-based irrigation system that integrates soil moisture monitoring using multiple sensors, edge computing, weather forecast data, automated control, and local data management. Four capacitive sensors connected to an STM32F103C8T6 collect data, while a BTT Pi V1.2 edge platform handles decision-making, weather data retrieval, and database management, thereby separating low-level measurement from higher-level control. During a one-month radish growing period (June 13 to July 13, 2026), 38,112 aggregated measurement cycle data records were collected. The median soil moisture level averaged 47.93% (standard deviation 3.84 points, range 32.2–54.0%). Three irrigation events resulted in a planned total volume of 26.68 L (calculated by the controller, not physically measured); the two events with a valid pre-irrigation reference value showed an increase in moisture of approximately 12.5 percentage points shortly after irrigation. These results demonstrate the technical feasibility of integrating environmental measurements, weather data, local decision-making, physical control, and continuous data collection into a single embedded irrigation platform, thereby laying the foundation for future analyses and next-generation precision agriculture systems.
