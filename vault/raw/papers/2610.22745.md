Abstract
Language models that read financial news are usually trained on text from the same years used to test them, so a backtest can reward hindsight. We build news topics from NoLBERT, an encoder pretrained only on text published between 1976 and 1995, adapted into a sentence encoder by contrastive fine-tuning on passages of the same article, with no labels or returns. We compare it with LDA, MiniLM and FinBERT readers on 1.26 million articles from 2012 to 2024, convert each reader’s topics into attention shocks and stock exposures, and forecast the returns of the 1,000 largest US stocks with eight models at three rebalancing frequencies. Models are fitted on 2013–2019, choices are made on 2020–2021, and 2022–2024 is held out. The experiment with the best validation score loses money at every frequency. Measuring attention shocks against the past 63 trading days instead of 5 raises the test rank IC of every reader. NoLBERT gives the most stable topics and forecasts about as well as LDA. An equal-weight combination of all readers and models earns a monthly net Sharpe ratio of 1.12, significant against zero before adjusting for the search but not against a price-only benchmark.
Keywords:
narrative asset pricing; NoLBERT; look-ahead bias; contrastive fine-tuning;
financial news; return prediction
1  Introduction
Financial news carries information about the economy, about firms and about the events that move prices, and large news archives
(
Dong et al., 2024
)
now make it possible to test whether that information forecasts returns. Transformer encoders can read such archives with some sense of context, placing two articles about the same event close together even when they share few words
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
. They also bring a problem into historical research. An encoder released in 2021 has read text written during most of any sample that ends before then, so the vector it gives a 2018 article already reflects what happened after 2018
(
Kakhbod and Li, 2025
)
. Holding out a test period for the return model does not remove this, because the hindsight sits in the features.
How much this matters depends on the model and the task. Language models can recall economic data from their training period
(
Lopez-Lira et al., 2025
)
, and their forecasts of events inside that period can be biased upwards
(
Sarkar and Vafa, 2024
)
. For news-based return prediction,
He et al. (2025)
find the bias to be modest. The same question applies to topic models and vocabularies estimated on the full sample.
Our earlier study
(
Prakash et al., 2026
;
Foley et al., 2026
)
compared LDA with a frozen sentence transformer in the narrative asset-pricing framework of
Bybee et al. (2023)
and found the transformer ahead on both coherence and Sharpe ratio, though the difference in performance was not statistically significant. That design had weaknesses that made the result hard to carry out of sample: topic models and vocabularies were fitted on the full sample, the stock universe used information not yet available at the start of each year, some choices were made on the evaluation months, and portfolios were evaluated before trading costs. This paper repeats the comparison under a stricter protocol. Related theoretical work highlights how heterogeneous trading decisions and market structure affect trading activity and welfare
Back et al. (2023)
.
The central addition is a point-in-time encoder. NoLBERT
(
Kakhbod and Li, 2025
)
was pretrained only on text published between 1976 and 1995, long before our news begins, but its raw output is not suited to comparing whole articles. We adapt it by contrastive fine-tuning on pairs of passages from the same article
(
Gao et al., 2021
;  as in)
, without returns or labels, cluster the adapted embeddings into topics, and compare these topics with LDA, MiniLM and a FinBERT topic classifier. All six readers label the same 1.26 million articles from 2012 to 2024 and are fitted on text up to 2019. Following
Bybee et al. (2023)
, we turn each reader’s topics into daily attention, attention shocks and stock exposures to those shocks, add firm-level news and price characteristics, and forecast the relative returns of the 1,000 largest US stocks with eight models, from least squares and IPCA to tree ensembles and the explainable boosting machine (EBM) of
Bell et al. (2026b)
. The forecasts are traded under nine portfolio rules at monthly, biweekly and weekly frequencies, net of costs. Models are fitted on 2013–2019, every choice is made on 2020–2021, and 2022–2024 is used only for reporting.
The results come in four parts. First, the adapted NoLBERT gives the most stable and most even topics of the six readers and forecasts about as well as LDA, the strongest of the readers trained on recent text. We find no evidence that the point-in-time restriction costs forecasting power, although the test cannot rule out moderate differences between readers. Second, how attention is measured matters more than which reader measures it: comparing attention with its mean over the past 63 trading days instead of the past week raises the test rank IC of every reader at every frequency. Third, choosing one experiment on the validation period fails. The experiment with the best validation IC loses money at every frequency, and opening the EBM shows why. An equal-weight combination of all readers and models with the 63-day window earns net Sharpe ratios of 1.12 (decile) and 1.19 (buffered) monthly. These are significant against zero before any adjustment for the size of the search, but they do not beat a price-only ensemble, and no single experiment survives a false-discovery correction. Fourth, we document problems in the news and return data that can create spurious predictability, among them article bodies attached to the wrong headline, a news source collected after the fact, and missing returns that depend on the future.

2  Related Work
Text and asset prices
Research that turns documents into numbers and relates them to economic outcomes is surveyed by
Gentzkow et al. (2019)
. In finance, early work measured the tone of a daily news column and found that pessimism predicts lower market returns, later reversed, and higher trading volume
(
Tetlock, 2007
)
.
Ke et al. (2019)
learn the words that predict returns directly, so the text representation is supervised by the outcome. We keep returns out of every text model, since our comparison is between representations.
Bybee et al. (2023)
fit LDA to the
Wall Street Journal
, turn the attention each topic receives into shocks, and use stocks’ covariances with those shocks as characteristics in a factor model. Our pipeline follows theirs up to the exposures and then forecasts returns with a set of models.
Chen et al. (2022)
use representations from large language models to predict the cross-section of returns, and we include their embedding regression as one of our feature sets.
A broader literature uses textual information to measure
economic conditions and predict financial outcomes.
baker2016measuring
construct a news-based
measure of economic policy uncertainty, while
manela2017news
and
calomiris2019news
show how information extracted from news can predict
market risk and returns. Related work applies
computational linguistics to central bank communication
(
hansen2018transparency
)
.
Our study contributes to this literature by examining
how alternative text representations, particularly
a historically pretrained language model, affect
the measurement of narrative attention and
out-of-sample return prediction.
Text-based measurement has spread across finance.
Kakhbod et al. (2026)
extract latent dimensions from Federal Reserve records,
Kakhbod et al. (2025b)
and
Hochberg et al. (2026)
build measures from patent text,
Kakhbod et al. (2025a)
study financial content on social media, and
Chen et al. (2023)
study how process intangibles shape agency conflicts. These corpora are written by different authors and on different schedules from daily newswire copy, and none of them produces the daily attention series that the narrative framework needs.
Topic models and sentence encoders
LDA represents a document as a mixture of topics estimated from word counts, so word order carries no information
(
Blei et al., 2003
)
. Sentence encoders summarize a passage in one vector that can be compared with others by cosine similarity
(
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019
)
. MiniLM, the encoder of our earlier study, is a small distilled model of this kind
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
, and FinBERT adapts BERT to financial text
(
Huang et al., 2023
)
. Our adaptation of NoLBERT uses the contrastive objective of
Gao et al. (2021)
, in which two views of the same text are pulled together and other texts in the batch are pushed apart. We judge topics partly by NPMI coherence
(
Röder et al., 2015
)
, which depends on how each topic’s top words are chosen, so we use one rule for every reader.
Look-ahead in language models
A model trained on text written during the sample can carry later information into the features of a backtest.
Lopez-Lira et al. (2025)
show that large language models recall economic and financial data from their training period,
Sarkar and Vafa (2024)
document look-ahead bias in pretrained models used for forecasting, and
He et al. (2025)
train models on chronologically restricted text and find that the bias in news-based return prediction is modest. NoLBERT addresses the problem by pretraining only on text from 1976 to 1995
(
Kakhbod and Li, 2025
)
. A related concern is that generated text depends on the prompt and can carry systematic biases
(
Fedyk et al., 2026
)
, which is one reason we cluster fixed embeddings instead of asking a generative model to label topics.
Forecasting and evaluation
We use the machine-learning approach to return prediction of
Gu et al. (2020)
and include IPCA, in which characteristics drive time-varying factor loadings
(
Kelly et al., 2019
)
. Interpretable models have been used to study corporate bond returns
(
Bell et al., 2026b
)
, portfolio choice
(
Bell et al., 2026a
)
and mortgage lending
(
Bell et al., 2025
)
, and we use the EBM of
Bell et al. (2026b)
to see which features drive a forecast. Combining forecasts is often more reliable than selecting one
(
Rapach et al., 2010
)
. Because we run hundreds of experiments per frequency, we follow
Harvey et al. (2016)
in treating single significant results with suspicion and report deflated Sharpe ratios
(
Bailey and López de Prado, 2014
)
and tests of the best result in the search
(
White, 2000
;
Hansen, 2005
)
.
3  Data
We combine financial news from 2012 to 2024 with the daily returns of the largest US stocks. The design follows our earlier study
(
Prakash et al., 2026
;
Foley et al., 2026
)
with three changes: a longer news sample with repaired text, a complete return file, and a strict order in time. Topic models see text up to 2019, every model choice is made on 2020–2021, and results are reported for 2022–2024.
3.1  News
The list of articles comes from FinMultiTime
(
Xu and others, 2025
)
, a collection of Nasdaq.com articles filed under the ticker pages on which they appeared. The text comes from FNSPID
(
Dong et al., 2024
)
, which covers the same articles up to 2023. For 93 tickers FinMultiTime pairs the correct headline and date with the body of an unrelated article, often one written years later. This affects 7.3% of the articles of 2012–2023, and left unrepaired it would leak later language into a vocabulary meant to end in 2019. FNSPID does not cover 2024, so for that year we use FinMultiTime’s text where it is intact and the headline alone otherwise.
We keep articles with a body of at least 400 characters, merge duplicates, and drop 16 December 2023, when the source holds a dump of tens of thousands of rows. This leaves 1,255,289 articles (Figure
1

To keep market-wide attention comparable across years, all topic models label the same random sample of at most 150 articles per day (529,657 articles) and are fitted on at most 60 per day dated up to 2019 (140,209 articles). The 2024 articles come from a second collection with fewer tickers and no headlines, so we also report 2022–2023 and 2024 separately.
Firm-level features use the articles filed under at most three tickers. The source was collected from ticker pages at the end of the sample and is not point-in-time. Companies acquired during the sample have no articles, and heavily covered firms keep only their most recent ones. We treat a firm with no article in the previous 126 trading days as not covered, which is the case for 50%, 39% and 40% of stock-months in the training, validation and test periods.

3.2  Returns
Returns and market values come from the CRSP daily and monthly stock files
(
Center for Research in Security Prices, 2026
)
, and the Fama–French factors and risk-free rate from the Kenneth R. French Data Library
(
French, 2026
)
. The universe consists of the 1,000 largest ordinary common shares (share codes 10 and 11 on NYSE, AMEX and Nasdaq) by market value at the end of each June from 2010 to 2024, and each list is used from that June until the next. We hold every daily return of the 2,062 stocks that enter a list, from 2011 to 2024. Portfolios are formed at the close of the last trading day of each month, fortnight or week. A stock enters a period if it is on the current list and has traded in the last five days, which gives about 984 stocks per period and panels of 141,761 stock-months, 306,746 stock-fortnights and 614,525 stock-weeks. A stock that stops trading during a holding period earns zero for the rest of it. The return file matters. From 2017 on, an earlier extract held daily returns only for stocks on the current calendar year’s June list, so from January to June a stock about to leave the list had no recent returns, and such stocks went on to underperform by 3% to 5% a month. A tree model learned to find them, and the resulting weekly net Sharpe ratio of 1.56 on price features alone came from this gap. The complete file removes this, and no stock in our panels lacks a recent return.
3.3  Sample Splits
The training period runs from 2013 to 2019 (84 months, 182 fortnights, 365 weeks), validation from 2020 to 2021 (24, 52, 104) and the test period from 2022 to 2024 (36, 78, 156). A holding period belongs to the split in which it starts. Topic models, vocabularies and cluster centroids are fitted on text up to 2019 and then applied unchanged. Hyperparameters and the attention-shock window are chosen on the validation period only. Each forecaster is then refitted on 2013–2021 and its test forecasts come from that frozen model.
4  Methods
4.1  Pipeline
Figure
2
shows the pipeline. A topic model, which we call a reader, gives every article a weight on each of its topics. Averaging the weights over a day’s articles gives each topic’s share of attention, and the gap between that share and its recent mean is the topic’s attention shock. The covariance of a stock’s returns with a topic’s shocks over the past year is the stock’s exposure to that narrative
(
Bybee et al., 2023
)
. These exposures, the composition and tone of the firm’s own news, and eight price characteristics are the features. Eight forecasting models turn them into forecasts of next period’s relative return, and nine portfolio rules turn the forecasts into portfolios. Everything after the reader is identical across readers, so differences in the results come from the text representation.
Our earlier study compared LDA with a frozen sentence transformer inside a single Sparse IPCA pricing model
(
Prakash et al., 2026
;
Foley et al., 2026
)
. Here we add a point-in-time language model adapted to financial news, use a set of forecasters in place of the single pricing model, and trade the forecasts at three rebalancing frequencies.

4.3  A Point-in-Time Encoder: NoLBERT
Two of our readers are built on encoders trained on text from inside our sample. MiniLM was released in 2021, and the FinBERT topic classifier was fine-tuned on financial text released in 2022, which falls in our test period. LDA is fitted on our own articles up to 2019 and has not read later text. NoLBERT
(
Kakhbod and Li, 2025
)
offers a pretrained encoder without this problem (Figure
3
a). It is a DeBERTa-v3 base model with twelve layers, hidden size 768, twelve attention heads and about 109 million parameters, trained to predict masked words in text published between 1976 and 1995, with 1996 held out for validation. Its tokenizer, with 30,000 word pieces, was also trained on that corpus, so no later text enters through the vocabulary, and position enters only through relative attention.
Kakhbod and Li (2025)
report that the model scores at least as well as FinBERT on a subset of the GLUE benchmark and that its masked-word probabilities favour 1976–1995 phrasing over both later and earlier phrasing.
The restriction has a cost. A vocabulary built on older text splits current financial language into more pieces, 1.49 tokens per word on our articles against 1.43 for MiniLM, so the same 64-token window covers less of each article. A larger problem is that the pretrained model has no sentence-level objective. When we average its token vectors with padding masked, two unrelated articles from our test period have a mean cosine similarity of 0.53, against 0.59 for a headline and its own lead. Vectors this close together are hard to cluster into 60 topics, which is why we adapt the encoder before using it.

4.4  Adapting NoLBERT by Contrastive Fine-Tuning
We fine-tune NoLBERT so that passages from the same article end up close together, following the unsupervised objective of
Gao et al. (2021)
. No head is added and no labels are used; the article boundary is the only supervision. There is no labelled topic assignment for 1.26 million articles, and a label set chosen by us would fix the topics in advance.
Pairs
We split each article published up to the end of 2019, the end of our training period, into passages, drop passages that occur verbatim in more than one article (mostly publisher boilerplate), and draw one pair of different passages per article, so that an article’s partner passage never appears as a negative for another pair in the same batch. This gives 230,931 pairs, of which 2,048 are held out. Passages are cut at 128 tokens. No returns, prices or text from the validation and test periods enter at this stage.

4.8  Combinations
Forecasts are combined by averaging their within-period ranks with equal weights, which requires no estimation
(
Rapach et al., 2010
)
. The ensemble of a feature set averages its eight base models. The combination across readers averages the ensembles of the six readers for one kind of feature set (price + text, or text alone) and one shock window, so it pools 48 fitted models. The price ensemble, the ensemble on price alone, is the benchmark without text. We also average the price ensemble with the single tone signal that has the best validation IC, and the ten base-model experiments with the best validation IC.
For comparison we report, within each family of experiments (a kind of feature set, a reader, the 63-day sets, the ensembles and so on), the experiment with the highest validation IC. The “overall pick” is the best of all base-model experiments of the main design.
4.9  Portfolios
Portfolios are formed at the close of each formation date and held to the next. The decile long-short buys the top tenth of forecasts and sells the bottom tenth with equal weights. The buffered long-short enters a stock in the top (bottom) tenth and holds it until it leaves the top (bottom) fifth, following the hold-out bands of
Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016)
. Both also appear with legs weighted by market value. The other rules are a rank-weighted portfolio of all stocks, dollar-neutral mean-variance weights under a five-factor covariance, the top decile alone with equal or market weights, and a market-weighted portfolio tilted towards the forecast; the last three are measured against the universe. Turnover is half the sum of absolute weight changes, and trading costs 10 basis points per dollar traded.

6  Discussion and Limitations
What a point-in-time encoder costs
Adapting NoLBERT took 150 minutes on one consumer GPU and no labels. The result is a reader whose pretraining text ends in 1995, whose topics are the most stable of the six, and whose forecasts are close to those of LDA and higher on average than those of MiniLM, an encoder trained on later text. We read this as evidence that the point-in-time restriction is affordable here. It is not evidence that it improves forecasts, and with three years of test data a moderate difference between readers in either direction would go undetected. The comparison with MiniLM also mixes two changes, the older pretraining text and our adaptation, which an adapted MiniLM run under the same recipe would separate.
Measuring attention
The window over which attention surprises are measured mattered more than the reader that measured them. A shock against the past quarter picks out topics that are gaining a lasting share of the news, and stock exposures to these slower shifts forecast returns in both halves of the test period, including the 2024 change of news source. The window was chosen on the validation period by a rule set in advance, but its advantage there was small, and we tried only four windows. The size of the test-period gain should therefore be read with the multiple-testing results in mind.
Choosing versus combining
A short validation period combined with flexible models produced the worst outcome of the study: the experiment with the best validation IC lost money at every frequency. The EBM’s decomposition located the problem in pairwise terms built on a feature that is mostly imputed. Averaging over readers and models gave steadier results, as
Rapach et al. (2010)
find for forecasts of the equity premium, and trading less through the buffer raised net returns in most experiments.
Limitations
The first limit is power. The test period has 36 months, 78 fortnights and 156 weeks, and the standard error of an annual Sharpe ratio is about 0.58 at every frequency, so only a true Sharpe ratio of about 1.7 or more would be detected four times out of five. Differences between readers, and between the combinations and the price ensemble, are not significant. The second is the search. Each frequency involves about 600 experiments and nine portfolio rules, no experiment passes a false-discovery threshold, and the deflated Sharpe ratios are small. The combinations across readers were formed after the main experiments had been run, so their
p
p
-values do not account for that choice.
The text models have their own limits. NoLBERT was adapted only on articles published up to the end of 2019, but MiniLM and the FinBERT classifier were trained on text from inside our sample. No reader’s topics are refitted after 2019, and their coherence on later text is about a third lower. The news source was collected at the end of the sample: it lacks companies acquired during the sample and keeps only the recent articles of heavily covered firms, so firm-level news is imputed for 40% to 50% of stocks, and the 2024 articles come from a second collection with fewer tickers and no headlines. Finally, trading costs are a flat 10 basis points per dollar traded, with no borrowing costs for short positions and no price impact, the universe is limited to the 1,000 largest stocks, and each experiment is run with a single seed.
7  Conclusion
We built news topics from an encoder that has read nothing published after 1995 and tested them against readers trained on recent text, in a design where every model choice precedes the test period. The point-in-time encoder gave stable topics and forecasts comparable to the best alternative. Measuring attention shocks over a quarter improved forecasts for every reader, and combining readers and models produced the strongest portfolios, with a monthly net Sharpe ratio of 1.12. That result is significant against zero before adjusting for the search, but it does not beat price-based forecasts and its deflated Sharpe ratio is small. A longer test period, a modern encoder adapted under the same recipe, and topics refitted over time would sharpen these comparisons.
