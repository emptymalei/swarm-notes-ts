Abstract
Time series forecasting models are increasingly deployed in continuously evolving environments. After a forecast is issued, future observations gradually arrive and reveal how the model has failed. Existing methods mainly treat such observations as supervision for parameter updates. However, it remains unclear whether verified forecasting errors can themselves provide causal feedback for correcting future predictions. To address this, we introduce Error Feedback Calibration (EFC), a fully test-time framework for deterministic post-hoc correction of time series forecasting. EFC constructs a Causal Error Diagonal (CED) from revealed errors of overlapping historical forecasts, providing a fresh view of the frozen forecaster’s recent failure behavior. Then Cumulative Error Transport (CET) maps this error state to future residuals using cumulative estimation, while temporal out-of-sample validation controls the correction strength. Temporal Residual Learning (TRL) further predicts only the residual structure left after CET from recent CED trajectories, rather than relearning the entire forecasting error. Extensive experiments across forecasting backbones and datasets show that EFC broadly improves forecasting performance while substantially controlling harmful over correction.

Time series forecasting, test-time adaptation, error feedback, post-hoc forecast correction.

can weaken patterns learned from historical data

. As the test stream evolves, a previously accurate forecaster may develop systematic errors, such as persistent bias and delayed responses to changing trends
[

. A model that performs well under offline evaluation may therefore degrade during deployment
[

. This motivates the need for forecast adaptation or correction as the underlying data evolves
[
14
]
.
To address such performance degradation during deployment, recent studies have explored post-hoc forecast revision
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
and test-time adaptation
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Post-hoc methods revise the forecasts after they are generated
[
15
,
21
,
22
]
, while test-time adaptation typically updates model parameters
[
18
]
or lightweight adapter
[
19
]
as the test stream evolves. Forecasting has an important temporal property: historical predictions are progressively verified as their outcomes become available
[
23
]
. Existing methods use this information to decide which forecasts should be revised or which model components should be updated
[
24
]
. The revealed forecasting error, however, provides information beyond the new observation itself. A new observation describes what happened in the data stream, whereas the error of an earlier forecast reveals how the forecaster’s prediction deviated from the observed outcome
[
17
]
. Once a forecast is verified, its error therefore becomes a direct record of the forecaster’s failure behavior
[
25
]
. This suggests a different perspective: revealed forecasting errors can be treated not only as signals for adaptation, but also as observations of how the forecaster has recently failed.
This perspective raises a central question: can forecasting errors that have already occurred and been verified during deployment serve as causal feedback for correcting future errors of a frozen forecaster? Fig.
1
illustrates how overlapping forecasts provide verified error feedback during deployment. Because multi-step forecasts are repeatedly issued from overlapping origins, a newly arrived observation can simultaneously verify several predictions made earlier and at different lead times
[
7
]
. These revealed errors are already available before the next forecast is issued, providing temporally valid feedback on the forecaster’s recent prediction errors. Turning revealed errors into useful corrections, however, is not straightforward
[
26
]
. First, the complete error of the current forecast is unavailable until its full prediction horizon has elapsed. Relying only on the latest fully matured error therefore introduces time delay. The first challenge is to organize these revealed cross-origin errors into a timely representation of the current forecasting error state. Second, recent errors cannot be assumed to persist unchanged into the future. Their relationship with future residuals can be noisy and horizon dependent, and directly reusing these errors may lead to over correction. The second challenge is therefore to estimate this relationship and assess the reliability of the resulting correction during deployment. Third, it remains unclear whether residual left after the initial correction contains temporal structure that can be predicted from recent error histories. A practical error feedback method must therefore observe the current error state in time, transport it reliably to future residuals, and identify what remains predictable afterwards.
Fig. 1:

Once its observation becomes available, their errors at different lead times are revealed. Rather than discarding these verified errors after evaluation, error feedback correction uses them to inform subsequent forecasts from a frozen forecaster.
To address these challenges, we propose Error Feedback Calibration (EFC), a fully test-time framework that uses already revealed forecasting errors to correct future predictions of a frozen forecaster. EFC first introduces the Causal Error Diagonal (CED), which organizes verified errors for the same target time from different historical forecast origins and lead times. CED provides a timely cross-origin view of the forecaster’s recent error behavior without waiting for the current forecast to mature. Based on this representation, Cumulative Error Transport (CET) estimates a stable mapping from revealed error states to future residuals using fully matured historical error pairs, while temporally out-of-sample reliability estimates control the correction strength. Temporal Residual Learning (TRL) then models only the residual left after CET from recent CED trajectories, rather than relearning the entire forecasting error. A separate temporally out-of-sample reliability estimate controls the strength of the TRL correction. Together, EFC uses verified historical errors to correct future forecasts while keeping the backbone frozen and excluding future observations unavailable at the current forecast origin. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We formulate verified forecasting errors as causal feedback and introduce the Causal Error Diagonal (CED) to organize revealed errors across historical forecast origins and lead times into a temporally available representation.
•
We develop Cumulative Error Transport (CET) to estimate a stable mapping from causal error states to future residuals using fully matured historical error pairs. CET further separates estimation from deployment through temporally out-of-sample reliability, so that an estimated correction is applied only when supported by independent historical evidence.
•
We also introduce Temporal Residual Learning (TRL) to predict the residual remaining after the CET correction actually issued at each historical origin, rather than relearning the full forecasting error. TRL uses recent CED trajectories to capture remaining temporal predictability and evaluates its incremental value with temporally out-of-sample evidence.
•
Extensive experiments across diverse real-world datasets and state-of-the-art backbones show that EFC improves frozen forecasting models across a broad range of settings with modest absolute overhead for most evaluated backbones.

Despite their architectural differences, these approaches primarily focus on improving the base forecasting mapping from historical observations to future values
[

. A stronger forecasting backbone may reduce average errors, but its error patterns can still change as the data evolve during deployment. More importantly, once forecasts have been issued and their errors are later revealed, backbone design alone does not specify how this newly verified information should be reused. Our work addresses this complementary problem. EFC keeps the forecasting backbone frozen and asks whether errors revealed during deployment can be reused as causal feedback to correct subsequent forecasts. It can therefore be applied on top of different forecasting architectures without redesigning or retraining the backbone.
II-B
Post-hoc Forecast Revision
Even with a strong forecasting backbone, individual predictions can still exhibit large errors
[
21
]
. This has motivated post-hoc forecast revision, which seeks to improve a forecast after the base prediction has already been produced
[
15
,
16
,
45
]
. Such methods are attractive because they can operate on existing forecasting models without redesigning the forecasting architecture. Recent work has explored identifying unreliable forecasting instances and revising them with local or global contextual information
[
15
]
. Other post-processing approaches refine the input or output interface of a deployed forecaster and learn lightweight corrections to its original predictions
[
12
,
16
]
. These studies show that forecast generation need not be the final step of the forecasting pipeline and that an issued prediction can still be improved through model-agnostic post-processing.
EFC shares this model-agnostic perspective but differs in how the correction signal is constructed. Existing post-hoc methods mainly focus on the current forecast and ask whether, or how, it should be revised using contextual or prediction-side information
[
46
]
. EFC instead looks back at forecasts issued earlier and uses the errors that have since been verified by real observations. It organizes these revealed errors as causal feedback about the recent behavior of the frozen forecaster and uses them to correct subsequent predictions. EFC therefore shifts the focus from revising a forecast with external context to learning from the forecaster’s own verified deployment errors.
II-C
Test-Time Adaptation for Time Series Forecasting
Test-time adaptation (TTA) has recently been introduced into time series forecasting to address distribution shifts that arise after deployment
[
14
]
. Unlike many conventional TTA settings, time series forecasting progressively reveals target observations that can provide supervision for online adaptation
[
18
]
. TAFAS
[
19
]
uses partially observed ground truth and gated calibration modules to adapt a frozen forecasting backbone. Subsequent work has focused on making this adaptation more efficient and more targeted. PETSA
[
18
]
updates only lightweight low-rank calibration modules at the input and output sides of the forecaster, reducing the cost of test-time adaptation. COSA
[
20
]
further moves adaptation into the output space, where a lightweight adapter directly corrects predictions using the base forecast and statistics from recently observed ground truth. Together, these studies show that newly available observations can be used to improve forecasting models during deployment without fully retraining the backbone.
EFC operates in the same deployment setting but assigns a different role to verified forecasting errors. Existing TTA methods in TSF mainly use newly revealed observations as supervision or context for updating model components or calibration functions
[
47
]
. EFC instead treats the forecasting errors produced by previously issued forecasts as the feedback state itself. It organizes errors that have already been verified across overlapping forecast origins, and learns how this error feedback relates to subsequent residuals while keeping the forecasting backbone frozen. Moreover, EFC separates immediate error state sensing from delayed parameter learning: current revealed errors can describe the recent behavior of the forecaster, whereas CET and TRL are updated only from fully matured historical forecasts. EFC therefore complements TSF-TTA by explicitly modeling verified forecasting errors as causal feedback for future correction.

IV-E
2
Cumulative Transport
To analyze the stability of error transport estimation during deployment, we compare different online estimators while keeping the same CED representation: Online SGD, which updates the transport relation through single-step gradient optimization; Sliding RLS, which estimates the relation using a fixed-size recent error window; Forgetting RLS, which gradually discounts older observations through a forgetting factor; and Cumulative RLS, which continuously incorporates all matured error observations. These estimators are evaluated based on correction gain, win rate, and numerical stability. We report correction gain relative MSE change compared with the frozen forecaster, where negative values indicate improvement. And numerical stability indicates whether the estimator produces stable updates without divergence or excessive oscillation during deployment. As shown in Table
VIII
, Online SGD fails to provide reliable correction and introduces additional errors, with a positive MSE increase of 26.40% and zero win rate, indicating that direct online updates are sensitive to noisy deployment feedback. Sliding RLS performs better than Online SGD but still increases MSE by 7.11% relative to the frozen backbone and exhibits oscillating updates. Forgetting RLS achieves stable correction, while cumulative RLS further improves both effectiveness and consistency, reducing MSE by 9.72% with an 80.9% win rate. These results demonstrate that accumulating matured error observations provides a more stable basis for estimating the error transport relationship under evolving deployment conditions.
The estimator comparison also clarifies the role of cumulative estimation under non-stationarity. CET does not model the observed series itself; it estimates a mapping from revealed forecasting errors to future residuals. Even as the data evolve, the CED input changes with the forecaster’s recent error state, while cumulative estimation pools fully matured historical pairs to reduce sensitivity to noisy updates. This is useful when the error-transport relation remains sufficiently persistent, consistent with the stability observed in our experiments. If that relation changes abruptly, however, the cumulative estimate may respond slowly. Temporal out-of-sample reliability controls the strength of the resulting correction rather than making the estimator forget previous samples.

Forecasting Visualization
To provide a more intuitive and comprehensive view of the effect of EFC, we present forecasting visualizations comparing the predictions of backbone models with and without EFC across three representative datasets. As illustrated in Fig.

On Traffic, forecasting errors are more pronounced around sharp peaks and their subsequent declines. EFC improves the alignment of several peak regions, although some local discrepancies remain. On Weather, the frozen predictions exhibit a sustained downward deviation from the observed trajectory, while EFC reduces this bias across the three backbones. These examples illustrate that error feedback can support different forms of forecast correction, ranging from local adjustments to more persistent bias correction. By using previously verified forecasting errors, EFC refines the predicted trajectories without modifying the underlying backbone.
V
Conclusion
In this work, we investigate verified forecasting errors as a source of feedback for correcting future predictions during deployment. Although modern forecasting models and foundation models provide strong predictive capabilities, their historical errors revealed during deployment still contain useful information for improving future predictions. To exploit this information without modifying the pretrained forecaster, we propose Error Feedback Calibration (EFC), a lightweight post-hoc correction framework that constructs causal error feedback signals, learns stable error transport, and models residual error dynamics through dedicated correction components. Extensive experiments on diverse real-world benchmarks demonstrate that EFC broadly improves forecasting accuracy while introducing limited computational overhead. In the future, we will investigate more general error feedback mechanisms for probabilistic forecasting, adaptive forecasting systems, and broader sequential prediction tasks.
