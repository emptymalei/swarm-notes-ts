Abstract
Lyapunov exponents (LEs) are fundamental metrics from nonlinear dynamical systems theory that quantify the average exponential rate of divergence or convergence of nearby trajectories in phase space. Over the past three decades, LEs have emerged as powerful tools across a wide range of power system problems, including transient and voltage stability assessment, chaos detection and mitigation, short-term load and renewable energy forecasting, and fault diagnosis. This paper presents a comprehensive review of these applications, covering more than 165 publications spanning from the early demonstrations of chaos in simplified machine models (1990s) to the most recent data-driven and machine-learning-augmented approaches applied to inverter-based resource (IBR)-dominated grids (2024–2026). The review is organized into four principal application domains: (i) stability analysis and real-time monitoring, (ii) chaos characterization and control, (iii) forecasting and predictability analysis, and (iv) protection, fault diagnosis, and condition monitoring. For each domain, we trace the evolution of methods, compare model-based and data-driven LE estimation strategies, identify key benchmarks and validation gaps, and outline the
most pressing open research problems.

Motivation and Context
Modern power systems are undergoing the most profound structural transformation since the introduction of alternating current. The rapid integration of inverter-based resources (IBRs)-wind turbines, photovoltaic (PV) systems, and battery energy storage -is displacing conventional synchronous generators (SGs) and fundamentally altering the dynamic behavior of the grid. Declining synchronous inertia, reduced inherent damping, and increased stochastic variability from renewable generation all amplify the nonlinear characteristics of the network
[
1
,
2
,
3

. Classical small-signal stability analysis, based on linearization and eigenvalue computation around an operating point, was designed for a world of large, inertia-rich synchronous machines. It provides only local information and cannot capture the large-disturbance, global nonlinear behavior that increasingly governs system security
[
4
,
5
]
.
At the same time, the wide deployment of phasor measurement units (PMUs) and wide-area measurement systems (WAMS) has created an unprecedented volume of high-resolution, time-synchronized measurement data. This data creates an opportunity to move beyond model-dependent stability assessment toward real-time, measurement-driven methods that can respond to the grid’s actual dynamic state rather than a precomputed model of it
[
6
,
7
,
8

.
Lyapunov exponents (LEs) sit at the intersection of these two trends. As a tool from nonlinear dynamical systems theory, LEs quantify the average exponential rate at which two trajectories that are initially close diverge in the system’s phase space. A positive largest Lyapunov exponent (LLE) signals exponential divergence and is the mathematical signature of chaos and instability; a negative LLE signals exponential convergence and confirms asymptotic stability. Because LEs are intrinsically nonlinear metrics-they make no assumption of small perturbations or proximity to an equilibrium-they are naturally suited to the analysis of stressed, post-fault, and large-disturbance behavior. Furthermore, LEs can be estimated directly from time-series data using phase-space reconstruction, enabling a model-free implementation compatible with PMU-based monitoring architectures

This review focuses on applications of Lyapunov exponents (LEs), in the strict sense of (

The scope is intentionally restricted to studies in which LE computation plays a direct methodological role. Therefore, related but distinct areas are excluded, including Lyapunov function-based direct stability methods, transient energy functions, Lyapunov-based control synthesis without LE computation, purely bifurcation-based studies without LE analysis, converter-level LE studies not directly connected to power system behavior, and machine-learning stability methods that do not incorporate an LE component. This boundary is used to maintain a clear distinction between LE-based analysis and broader Lyapunov-theoretic or nonlinear-dynamics approaches in power engineering.

Cross-Cutting Themes
Several analytical distinctions recur across all four domains and are explicitly addressed in every section of this review.
Model-based vs. data-driven LE estimation.
Model-based approaches compute LEs from the variational equations of an analytical power system model
[
4
,
1
]
. They provide theoretical rigor and can be used for offline stability mapping, but require a validated model and are computationally intensive for large systems. Data-driven (model-free) approaches estimate LEs directly from measured time-series data using phase-space reconstruction
[
22
,
7
]
. They are compatible with PMU-based monitoring but are sensitive to noise, window length, and embedding parameters.
Largest LE vs. full Lyapunov spectrum.
Most power system applications use only the LLE (
λ
1
\lambda_{1}
) because its sign determines stability and instability. The full LE spectrum
{

Conventional SG grid vs. IBR-dominated grid.
Nearly all papers before 2020 analyze grids dominated by synchronous generators, for which the swing equation provides a well-understood nonlinear model. IBR dynamics are governed by inner control loops operating on microsecond timescales, producing qualitatively different phase-space behavior. Adapting LE methods to IBR-dominated grids is identified throughout this review as one of the most important open problems

1.
Unified framework across four domains.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first review to systematically cover stability assessment, chaos control, forecasting, and condition monitoring under a single LE-based framework, revealing cross-domain methodological parallels that are not visible when each application is surveyed in isolation.
2.
Explicit model-based vs. data-driven comparison.
We explicitly compare LE estimation pipelines across both paradigms in each application domain, identifying the trade-offs in accuracy, computational cost, noise sensitivity, and real-time deployability.
3.
Coverage of modern grid contexts.
The review includes the most recent literature on IBR-dominated grids, renewable integration uncertainty
[
1
]
, and hybrid ML-LE assessment methods
[
31
]
, contexts that existing surveys do not address.
4.
Critical gap analysis.
Each section concludes with an explicit identification of open problems, methodological limitations, and under-explored directions, providing a research agenda for the community.

Definition and Signatures of Chaos
A deterministic dynamical system governed by (

II-B
3
Chaos vs. Voltage Collapse
An important distinction, relevant to Section
III
, is between chaos and voltage collapse. Chaotic oscillations are
bounded
: the trajectory wanders on a strange attractor but does not diverge to infinity. Voltage collapse, by contrast, is an
unbounded
divergence of the trajectory away from the feasibility region. In some parameter regimes, the two phenomena are connected: a chaotic attractor can undergo a boundary crisis, in which it collides with an unstable manifold and suddenly disappears, after which the trajectory collapses
[
12
]
. This crisis mechanism provides a nonlinear-dynamics explanation of certain voltage-collapse events and motivates the use of LEs as early-warning indicators.

III-A

LLE as a Transient Stability Certificate
The theoretical basis for using LEs in transient stability analysis (TSA) was established in the model-based literature, which computes LEs directly from variational equations of the power system model rather than from measured data.

The framework was extended by Bosetti and Khan

This enables characterization of local stability structure near the stability boundary, including the detection of hyperbolicity and basin-boundary geometry in multi-machine oscillating systems.

III-B
PMU-Based Real-Time Rotor-Angle Stability Monitoring
III-B
1
Pioneer Work: Windowed LLE from Phasor Measurements
The shift from model-based offline analysis to measurement-based real-time monitoring was pioneered by Liu
et al.
[
23
]
, who proposed estimating the largest windowed Lyapunov exponent directly from real-time phasor measurements of rotor angle and frequency obtained from PMUs. The swing-equation parameters were identified via least-squares from the PMU data, and the variational equation was integrated over a sliding window of approximately 1,s. A positive windowed exponent was interpreted as evidence of a transiently chaotic power swing, i.e., a swing that is sensitively dependent on initial conditions and may lead to instability even though it does not immediately lose synchronism.
This early work established three ideas that persist throughout the subsequent literature: (i) the LLE can be estimated from PMU time series without a full system model; (ii) a finite window of 1–2,s is sufficient for a practical stability decision; and (iii) the positive-LLE criterion correctly identifies contingencies that will eventually become unstable before any overtly visible divergence.

III-F
Stochastic Stability and Uncertainty
Propagation
Classical LLE-based TSA and STVS methods treat disturbances

quantifies how uncertainty propagates through the nonlinear
grid dynamics and affects stability margins.

III-G
Optimal PMU Placement for Stability
Monitoring
Effective deployment of PMU-based LLE monitoring requires
identifying which buses must be instrumented to maximize
the stability-monitoring information content of the
measurement system.
Rashidi and Farjah

A complementary approach by Rashidi

Yoon

[

rate (most dynamically vulnerable), and DGs are sited
at these buses to reduce the positive LLE magnitude.
The approach avoids full dynamic modeling and provides
a data-driven, dynamics-aware DG placement criterion.
III-H
Hybrid Machine Learning and LLE Methods
The most recent phase of the stability-assessment
literature (2020-present) is characterized by the
fusion of LLE-based nonlinear dynamics indicators with
machine-learning classifiers, motivated by two
complementary goals: improving classification speed
relative to standalone LLE methods, and improving
physical interpretability relative to pure deep-learning
approaches.
III-H

network topology changes and measurement noise, and
generalizes to unseen contingency types within the

Oyama

(1) Oscillation contamination in STVS.

(2) FTLE window selection.

(3) LLE under IBR dynamics.
The theoretical basis for LLE-based stability assessment
was developed for inertia-dominated SG systems. IBR
control loops operate on microsecond timescales and
introduce non-smooth switching dynamics (LVRT, current
limiters, mode transitions) that are fundamentally
incompatible with classical smooth Lyapunov theory.
The GVD/pGVD framework

for LLE-based TSA in fully IBR-dominated systems is

(4) Scalability to large real-world systems.
Most PMU-based LLE methods have been validated on
benchmark test systems of up to a few hundred buses.
Real-time deployment in systems with thousands of
PMUs, high measurement noise, communication latency,
missing data, and time-varying topology remains largely
undemonstrated. The THRUST validation on a 13,029-bus

is a notable exception

(5) Stability margin quantification.
Current LLE-based methods provide binary stable/unstable
decisions or, at best, a qualitative severity ordering.
A rigorous, quantitative stability margin
(analogous to the energy margin in direct methods or the
damping ratio in modal analysis) derived from the LLE
and validated against physical instability thresholds
remains an open problem.

IV-J
Open Problems in Chaos and Control
Despite the substantial literature reviewed above,
several critical gaps remain.
(1) IBR model realism.
The vast majority of chaos studies use simplified
integer-order or fractional-order ODE models of
synchronous generators. The dynamics of GFM and GFL
converters under large disturbances are governed by
control-loop switching and saturation, not swing-equation
nonlinearities. The hidden-attractor work
[
109
,
110
]
is the only systematic study that begins from a
converter-native model. More work is needed to determine
whether the control synthesis methods of
Sections
IV-F
–
IV-H
are applicable to IBR-dominated networks.
(2) Scalability from SMIB to multi-machine
networks.
Most chaos-control papers are validated on SMIB or
3-bus benchmark models. Extension to realistic large-scale
multi-machine systems requires addressing: (i) the
exponential growth of phase-space dimension with
system size; (ii) the interaction between multiple
simultaneously chaotic machines; and (iii) the need
for distributed rather than centralized controllers.
(3) From chaos suppression to chaos prevention.
All reviewed chaos-control methods operate
reactively
: they suppress chaos after it has
been detected. A proactive paradigm-using LLE-based
stability margins to detect bifurcation approach and
adjust operating parameters before chaos onset-is
largely absent. The LLE-based PMU monitoring tools of
Section
III
provide the detection
capability; the connection to preemptive control actions
is not yet made.
(4) Experimental and field validation.
Without exception, the chaos-control results in this
literature are validated by simulation on toy models.
No paper provides hardware experimental or field
validation of chaos suppression using an LLE-based
controller. Real-time implementation faces challenges
in LLE computation latency, noise robustness, and
closed-loop stability under digital control delays.
(5) Fractional-order model interpretability.
The physical interpretation of the fractional order
q
q
remains debated
[
48
]
. The

Islanding Detection Using Chaos Analysis
VI-A
1
Motivation and the Islanding Detection Problem
Islanding-the continued energization of a microgrid after it has been disconnected from the main grid-is a safety and power-quality hazard. Passive islanding detection methods based on voltage magnitude, frequency, and total harmonic distortion (THD) have well-known non-detection zones (NDZs): when the local microgrid generation closely matches its load, the grid-disconnection event causes no detectable electrical perturbation. Active methods inject disturbances to force a measurable response, but degrade power quality under normal operation. The chaos-based detection paradigm proposed in the literature offers a third path: exploit the observation that the nonlinear dynamics of the PCC voltage waveform change qualitatively between grid-connected and islanded modes, and use the LLE of the waveform as a passive discriminant that avoids both the NDZ problem and the power-quality cost.

(1) Adaptive embedding parameter selection.

(2) Robustness to measurement noise.

(3) Multi-class fault discrimination beyond LLE sign.
Several papers confirm that different fault types produce different LLE magnitudes but do not establish clear separation thresholds, and none provides a rigorous statistical decision framework for multi-class classification based on LLE alone. The three-invariant approach (LLE + CD + KE) is the most promising direction, but formal classifier design and cross-validation on large, diverse datasets are absent.
(4) Real-time and hardware-in-loop validation.
Most results are obtained from simulated or laboratory bench data. Field validation on live equipment at operating voltage and load levels-where measurement noise, electromagnetic interference, and installation artifacts are significant-is absent or only partially addressed (the Ge 2016 islanding paper uses RTLAB, which is the closest example).
(5) Fusion with complementary features.
The LLE captures trajectory divergence rate; correlation dimension captures fractal structure; Kolmogorov entropy captures information production. Approximate entropy, sample entropy, recurrence plots, and recurrence quantification analysis (RQA) carry complementary nonlinear information. Systematic fusion of these metrics in a principled multi-feature framework, with feature-importance analysis and dimensionality reduction, has not been done for any of the four application classes.
(6) IBR and power-electronics-heavy equipment.
All protection applications in the corpus concern conventional power-frequency signals (50/60 Hz) from traditional equipment. As converter-dominated microgrids, solid-state transformers, and wide-bandgap-device switchgear become prevalent, the signal characteristics change fundamentally: switching harmonics, high-frequency transients, and faster response timescales mean that the phase-space reconstruction and LLE estimation procedures designed for 50/60 Hz signals must be re-evaluated for these new equipment types.

VII-A
Lyapunov Exponents in IBR-Dominated Grids: The Fundamental Challenge
The single most consequential shift in the power-systems LE literature is the transition from synchronous-generator (SG)-dominated to IBR-dominated networks. This is not a quantitative refinement-it is a qualitative change in the mathematical structure of the problem.
VII-A
1
Why Classical LE Theory Needs Extension
Classical LE-based methods were developed for systems whose dynamics are well described by the swing equation (Section
II-A
). The LLE of the post-fault swing trajectory-whether computed from a model or estimated from PMU rotor-angle data-provides a physically grounded stability indicator precisely because the swing dynamics are smooth, autonomous, and governed by a well-defined attractor structure.
IBR dynamics violate each of these assumptions:
•
Non-smooth switching:
Grid-following (GFL) and grid-forming (GFM) converters switch between operating modes (normal, LVRT, current limiting) on microsecond timescales. The state trajectory is piecewise smooth, not continuously differentiable, invalidating the variational equation (
9
).
•
Virtual inertia:
GFM converters emulate inertia through software, not rotating mass. The effective inertia is frequency-dependent and control-law-defined, producing dynamics that may not resemble the swing equation in any operating regime.
•
Multiple coexisting attractors:
As shown in Section
IV
[
109
,
110
]
, islanded GFM microgrids can exhibit hidden attractors with fractal basin boundaries, making small-signal LLE (computed near the equilibrium) useless as a predictor of post-disturbance fate.
•
Black-box device models:
Modern IBRs are controlled by proprietary software; their differential equations are not available for model-based LLE computation.

Open Directions
The Koopman–LE connection suggests several important directions. VII-H
Open Problems Spanning All Domains
Synthesizing the domain-specific open problems from Sections

with the cross-cutting themes above, we identify seven overarching research priorities.
(1) A unified LE framework for IBR-dominated grids.
The GVD/pGVD framework addresses STVS; the SMLE addresses commutation-failure transients; hidden-attractor theory addresses microgrid sub-synchronous oscillations. A single, mathematically consistent framework covering all three phenomena for fully IBR-dominated networks (zero synchronous inertia) has not yet been established.
(2) Rigorous probabilistic stability margins.
The almost-sure LE theory provides a noise-intensity margin

(3) Real-time large-scale deployment.
No paper demonstrates continuous, 24/7 real-time LLE-based stability monitoring on a real utility system. Demonstration projects analogous to those that validated PMU-based angle monitoring on the WECC and European grids are needed.
(4) Cross-domain method transfer.
Methods developed for one domain are rarely tested in another. For example, the CE-based chaos feature of the PD identification paper
[
19

could potentially be applied to PMU voltage sequences for STVS; the wavelet-LLE pipeline of turbine fault diagnosis

(5) Explainability and operator trust.
LLE-based indices are physically interpretable in principle (trajectory divergence rate), but operators need not only an alarm but also an explanation of
which device or bus
is driving the instability and
what control action
is recommended. The pGVD decomposition

(6) LLE under cyber-physical attacks.
Yan
et al.
[
124

connected conditional Lyapunov exponent (CLEs) to cyber-intrusion risk, but the interaction between adversarial measurement manipulation and LLE estimation-specifically, can an attacker inject PMU data to fool a LLE-based stability monitor?- has not been studied.
(7) Standardized benchmarks.
Each paper in this corpus validates its method on a different test system, fault scenario, and LLE estimation procedure. A standardized benchmark (common test system, common contingency set, common LLE implementation, common accuracy metrics) analogous to the IEEE transient stability test suite would allow rigorous method comparison and tracking of field progress.

VIII-B
Principal Lessons Learned
Synthesizing the findings across all four domains, we identify six lessons that carry broad methodological significance.
Lesson 1: The finite-time LLE is the practical object, not the asymptotic LE.
The mathematical LE requires
t
→
∞
t\to\infty

The behavior of these estimates-their bias, variance, convergence rate, and sensitivity to oscillation, noise, and window length-is the central practical challenge of the entire field. Understanding this distinction is the prerequisite for all applied LE work.
Lesson 2: Model-based and data-driven methods are complements, not competitors.
Model-based LLE computation (GSR, discrete-QR) provides rigorous stability region mapping and theoretical guarantees but requires model fidelity and computational resources. Data-driven LLE estimation (Rosenstein, Kantz, online recursive) enables real-time deployment from PMU data but is sensitive to noise and embedding parameters. The most robust systems combine both: offline model-based analysis for baseline calibration, online data-driven monitoring for real-time decisions.
Lesson 3: The LLE alone is not sufficient; the LE spectrum and companion metrics add essential information.
Most papers use only the largest LE. The full LE spectrum determines the Kaplan-Yorke dimension, the metric entropy, and the sum-to-zero constraint (Liouville’s theorem). Companion metrics-correlation dimension

Lesson 4: Chaos verification requires more than a positive LLE.
The cautionary result of

Lesson 5: Physical interpretability is the key advantage of LE over pure data-driven methods-preserve it.
The LLE’s physical meaning (exponential trajectory divergence rate) is what distinguishes it from black-box stability classifiers. When LEs are combined with ML, this advantage should be preserved by using the LE to anchor physical meaning (as a feature, labeler, or constraint) rather than simply absorbing it into a deep-learning black box. VIII-C
Closing Remarks
The field reviewed in this paper has achieved something remarkable: a single mathematical concept from 1960s dynamical systems theory-the Lyapunov exponent-has been developed into a practical monitoring tool, a diagnostic instrument, a forecasting limit, and a fault-classification feature, all within the same engineering discipline. The breadth of application is matched by the depth of the theoretical foundation: from Oseledets’ ergodic theorem to Takens’ embedding theorem to Koopman operator theory, each layer of mathematics has found a practical role.
Yet the field stands at a pivotal moment. The power grid is transitioning from the synchronous-generator dynamics for which all existing LE methods were developed toward a converter-dominated regime whose physics are fundamentally different. The GVD/pGVD framework, the SMLE, and the hidden-attractor analysis are the first credible responses to this challenge, but they cover only fragments of the problem. The next decade of LE-based power system research will be defined by whether the community can build a coherent, theoretically grounded, and practically validated framework for Lyapunov analysis in IBR-dominated grids-one that preserves the physical interpretability that makes LEs uniquely valuable while adapting to a system whose dynamics no longer resemble the swing equation.
The 169 research priorities, methodological lessons, and terminological recommendations documented in this review are offered as a foundation for that effort.

Mohammad Almomani
is a Ph.D. candidate in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Iowa State University. His research focuses on nonlinear dynamics, power system stability, and data-driven stability assessment for modern power grids with increasing penetration of inverter-based resources. His interests include Lyapunov-exponent-based stability analysis, voltage stability, transient stability, oscillation detection, reactive power control, wide-area monitoring using PMU/WAMS data, transmission and distribution co-simulation, and transmission system operator–distribution system operator interaction. His recent work investigates how nonlinear dynamical-system tools, particularly Lyapunov exponents and phase-space reconstruction, can support stability monitoring, chaos characterization, renewable-energy forecasting, protection, and fault diagnosis in power systems. This research is motivated by the growing need for measurement-driven and model-free methods capable of assessing the dynamic behavior of low-inertia, renewable-rich grids. Almomani received the B.Sc. degree in electrical engineering from Yarmouk University in 2017 and the M.S. degree in electrical engineering from Mutah University in 2021.
