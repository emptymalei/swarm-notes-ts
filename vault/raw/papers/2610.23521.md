Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can now perceive, describe, and reason about images and audio, yet time series understanding remains underestimated: most work uses LLMs as forecasters and classifiers, reasoning studies are few and narrow, and no suite trains and evaluates the full range of capabilities. In this paper, we build the first such suite, with 337K training and 44K evaluation samples across three progressive capabilities, Perceive, Solve, and Reason, and use it to ask how time series should enter an LLM that must master all three, and in what order. We find that a dedicated encoder is essential: with one, a 4B model surpasses GPT-4o on every perception attribute we test, whereas textualizing the series costs 8–10 points in reasoning accuracy. The capabilities then build on one another: learning them in order beats mixing the same data and rivals reasoning-only training with over three times the reasoning data, but only when an encoder is used. Analyses further reveal that capabilities are transferred through distinct network depths rather than simply stacked, and that a stage converged on its own benchmark is not necessarily ready for the next. Distilled into a simple recipe, these findings yield 4B TS-LLMs that, without any target-domain data, outperform four recent 7B TS-LLMs on seven of eight out-of-domain metrics, by up to 11 points. We therefore advocate treating time series as a first-class modality for LLMs, read through dedicated encoders and taught to perceive before they reason. Code is available at
https://github.com/EIT-NLP/PSR
.

Introduction
Time series record how the world evolves, from power grids and patient vitals to markets and traffic, and reading them in context underlies many critical decisions
[
10
]
. In vision and audio, large language models (LLMs) have become general assistants that perceive, describe, and reason about their inputs, built on training and evaluation suites that span these capabilities
[
18
,
21
,
6
,
28
,
20
]
. Time series understanding, by contrast, remains underestimated. Most work on time series LLMs (TS-LLMs) applies them to traditional tasks such as forecasting, classification, and anomaly detection
[
38
,
11
,
16
,
14
]
, reducing understanding to curve fitting
[
26
]
. Studies of reasoning are few and narrow, often stopping at surface-level question answering
[
31
,
7
]
, and benchmarks for generalist models largely overlook time series
[
34
]
. Above all, no off-the-shelf suite trains and evaluates the full range of time series capabilities.
We view comprehensive time series understanding as three progressive capabilities:
perceiving
basic attributes such as trend, seasonality, and local patterns;
solving
standard tasks such as forecasting and anomaly detection; and
reasoning
about a series and its context to explain, extrapolate, and decide. Each capability has been studied on its own
[
2
,
37
,
9
,
17
,
13
,
30
,
22
]
, and so has the way time series enter the LLM: as text, as plotted images, or through a dedicated encoder with different alignment designs
[
19
,
32
,
8
,
31
,
26
]
. These comparisons, however, are each made in a separate setting with its own tasks and data. When one model must acquire all three capabilities, a question arises that isolated settings cannot answer:
how should time series enter the LLM, and in what order should the capabilities be learned?
The answer hinges on whether what is learned for one capability carries over to the next.
To answer this question, we build, to our knowledge, the first suite that trains and evaluates all three capabilities. It curates four public corpora, covering synthetic series and real-world series from over ten domains
[
31
,
13
,
27
,
26
]
. The data are organized into three stages, Perceive, Solve, and Reason, each with its own training set and held-out evaluation set, totaling 337K training and 44K evaluation samples. Three external benchmarks are reserved for out-of-domain testing
[
4
,
2
,
37
]
. On this suite, we compare textualization, which writes the series as text, with two encoder-based designs, pre-alignment and post-alignment, which align encoder outputs before or within the LLM
[
26
,
13
]
. For each design, we contrast
progressive
training, which learns the three stages in order, with
mixed
training on all the data at once and with reasoning-only training. We further examine which perceptual attributes matter, how gradients evolve under each training scheme, and how representations transfer across layers.
In this setting,
a dedicated encoder proves essential
. Textualization trails both encoder-based designs by 8–10 points in reasoning accuracy, although it fine-tunes the LLM while pre-alignment keeps the LLM frozen. Borrowed formats miss fine-grained structure. Even GPT-4o, reading a series as text or as a plot, scores about half as high as our perception-trained 4B models on local patterns and inter-variable relations. Ablating the perception data shows that these are exactly the attributes reasoning relies on most. Between the two alignment designs, the lighter pre-alignment performs on par with post-alignment. Its encoder can even be frozen after perception with little loss, so
later capabilities form mainly by re-aligning fixed temporal features rather than by refining the encoder
.
Given such an encoder, we reach four findings on how the capabilities form. (1)
The capabilities build on one another.
Perception alone improves all four standard tasks, and perception and task solving strengthen complementary aspects of reasoning: the former chiefly helps read the signal and ground it in context, the latter chiefly helps infer dependencies and choose actions. (2)
Order matters beyond the data, but only with an encoder.
On identical data, learning the stages in order beats mixing them, most of all when reasoning data are scarce: with progressive training,
15
%
15\%
of the reasoning data roughly matches reasoning-only training on
50
%
50\%
. Under mixed training, reasoning gradients dominate the updates while aligning poorly with the overall objective, and ordering the stages more than doubles this alignment. Without an encoder, however, the advantage nearly vanishes, an interaction that only a multi-capability setting can reveal. (3)
Capabilities are transferred through distinct network depths rather than simply stacked.
Perception-to-solving transfer dominates the shallower layers and solving-to-reasoning transfer the deeper ones, while direct perception-to-reasoning transfer stays weakest, so reasoning is built through task solving. (4)
Local convergence does not mean readiness.
Each preceding stage saturates on its own benchmark with about
70
%
70\%
of its data, yet training on the rest still benefits the stages that follow, so local convergence alone should not decide when to advance.
These findings translate into a simple recipe: we should encode time series with a dedicated encoder and learn the three stages in order, each on its full data. With only 4B parameters, the resulting TS-LLMs achieve the best result on seven of eight out-of-domain metrics, ahead of 7B TS-LLMs such as TimeOmni-1, ITFormer, ChatTS, and PATRA
[
7
,
30
,
31
,
22
]
.
In summary, our contributions are as follows: (1)
A comprehensive suite.
We build the first training and evaluation suite that covers perception, task solving, and reasoning for time series. (2)
How time series should enter the LLM.
In this multi-capability setting, we show that a dedicated encoder is essential, that a pre-aligned encoder trained once suffices, and that later capabilities form by re-alignment. (3)
How capabilities form.
We show that the capabilities build on one another and are best learned in order, explain this through gradient alignment, depth-wise representation transfer, and stage readiness, and turn the findings into a recipe for strong, compact TS-LLMs.

A Progressive Three-Stage Capability Dimensions for TS-LLMs
Despite recent progress in applying LLMs to time series perception, task solving, and reasoning, these capabilities are often studied independently. To analyze the relationships among different tasks, we organize the capabilities of TS-LLMs into three stages: Perceive, Solve, and Reason. More data details are provided in Appendix

&
C
.

Model Architecture.
Existing studies have developed three main paradigms in Figure

(1) Time Series Textualization
directly converts time series into textual sequences that can be processed by LLMs.
(2) Pre-align
architecture extracts temporal representations with an independent time series encoder and projects them into the LLM embedding space, enabling joint processing with text inputs.
(3) Post-align

Advanced time series analysis relies on understanding fundamental temporal dynamics. For example, forecasting requires capturing trend patterns, while anomaly analysis requires identifying local behaviors. Therefore, Stage 1 aims to equip LLMs with basic temporal perception. The questions focus on trends, seasonality, anomalies, local patterns, and multivariate relationships.
Training Set.
The training data is mainly collected from ChatTS, with approximately

Stage 2: Temporal Task Solving.
Many real-world time series applications require models to solve specific tasks. Therefore, Stage 2 investigates whether models can transfer basic temporal perception abilities to downstream task solving.
Training Set.
We use TSQA
[
4
]
and TS Instruct
[
27

TS Instruct provides an additional 2,945 classification samples, resulting in 158,159 samples in total. All samples are split into training and testing sets with an 8:2 ratio.
Evaluation Set.
We evaluate four standard tasks. Forecasting and imputation are evaluated using
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
, while anomaly detection and classification are evaluated using
Accuracy
.
Stage 3: High-level Reasoning.
Stage 3 aims to advance TS-LLMs to intelligent systems capable of analysis.
Training Set.
TSQA reasoning QA provides 26,163 samples, ChatTS TSEvol and Instruct Follow
[
31
]
provide 29,320 samples, the TSCognition

]
training set provides 32,869 samples, and TS Instruct provides 12,852 samples, resulting in 105,751 samples in total.
Evaluation Set.
We use two independent sets. Set A contains 4,547 samples covering binary, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions, evaluated using

, and
BLEU

7
Conclusion
Our work investigates how temporal capabilities progressively form in TS-LLMs. We organize model abilities into three stages: Perceive, Solve, and Reason, and show that ordered capability accumulation provides a stronger foundation for high-level reasoning than isolated or mixed training. Further analyses reveal that capability formation is influenced by training order, input format, stage maturity, and cross-layer representation transfer. We also find that local stage convergence does not necessarily indicate sufficient preparation for subsequent capability development. These observations provide a clearer understanding of how lower-level temporal abilities support more advanced reasoning. Based on these findings, we develop a progressive training strategy for TS-LLMs, which achieves strong out-of-domain performance and offers a practical pathway for building more comprehensive temporal reasoning models.

Appendix B
Baseline
ChatTS
[
31
]
.
ChatTS is a time series understanding framework that enhances LLMs with instruction-based temporal data covering fundamental patterns such as trends, seasonality, anomalies, and local variations. It converts time series into representations compatible with language models and performs instruction tuning for temporal question answering. We use ChatTS as a representative baseline for evaluating whether models trained on temporal understanding data can generalize to more comprehensive perception and reasoning tasks.
TimeOmni-1
[
7
]
.
TimeOmni-1 is a unified TS-LLM designed to support multiple time series understanding and reasoning tasks within a single framework. It integrates temporal information with language instructions and is trained on diverse time series tasks to improve cross-task generalization. We include TimeOmni-1 as a representative general-purpose TS-LLM baseline for comparing comprehensive temporal perception and reasoning capabilities.
ITFormer
[
30
]
.
ITFormer is an end-to-end multimodal framework for time series question answering that jointly models temporal representations and language generation. It directly integrates time series features with the language model to support temporal understanding and reasoning over multimodal inputs. We use ITFormer as a representative alignment-based TS-LLM baseline for evaluating high-level temporal reasoning and out-of-domain generalization.
PATRA
[
22
]
.
PATRA is a recent reasoning-oriented TS-LLM developed for complex temporal analysis and question answering. It emphasizes the integration of time series representations with language reasoning to improve performance on diverse temporal reasoning tasks. We include PATRA as a strong recent baseline for comparing the reasoning and generalization capabilities of our progressively trained TS-LLMs.
Qwen3-4B
[
33
]
.
Qwen3-4B is the base large language model used in our framework. We adopt it as a non-specialized LLM baseline by directly applying the same input format without progressive time series capability training. This baseline is used to evaluate the temporal understanding and reasoning ability provided by the pretrained language model itself, and to measure the gains introduced by our stage-wise Perceive

Appendix C
Dataset
ChatTS
[
31
]
.
ChatTS constructs fully synthetic time series–text data for temporal understanding and reasoning. Its training data consist of five subsets: UTS, MTS-Shape, MTS-Local, TSEvol, and Instruct Follow. UTS contains 35K samples for basic univariate temporal attributes, including global and local patterns; MTS-Shape contains 35K multivariate samples focusing on global trend correlations; and MTS-Local contains 35K samples emphasizing correlated local fluctuations across variables. In addition, TSEvol provides 24,270 diverse question–answer pairs for temporal reasoning, while Instruct Follow contains 5,050 samples for instruction-following ability. The generated time series cover lengths from 64 to 1024 and include numerical supervision such as extrema, segmented averages, local fluctuation positions and amplitudes, seasonality, trends, and point-wise values. In our framework, the three alignment subsets are mainly used for Stage 1 temporal perception, while TSEvol and Instruct Follow are incorporated into Stage 3 reasoning training.
TSQA
[
13
]
.
TSQA is a large-scale time series QA dataset introduced with Time-MQA, containing approximately 200K QA pairs across 12 real-world application domains, including healthcare, environment, energy, finance, transportation, IoT, AIOps, and human activities. It covers five task categories: forecasting, imputation, anomaly detection, classification, and open-ended reasoning. Specifically, it contains 42,557 forecasting, 38,657 imputation, 37,000 anomaly detection, 37,000 classification, and 37,629 open-ended QA samples. The reasoning subset further includes true/false, multiple-choice, and open-ended questions. In our framework, the numerical task subsets are mainly used in Stage 2, while the reasoning QA subset is incorporated into Stage 3.
TS Instruct
[
27
]
.
TS Instruct is a multimodal instruction-tuning dataset introduced with Chat-TS, pairing diverse time series with natural-language instructions and responses. The training set contains 18,306 samples covering four categories: 9,726 reasoning samples, 3,126 decision-making samples, 2,945 classification samples, and 2,509 mathematical reasoning samples. The dataset is generated using time series properties, visual representations, task descriptors, and synthetic conversations to support joint reasoning over temporal and textual information. In our framework, the classification subset is used in Stage 2, while the remaining reasoning-oriented samples are incorporated into Stage 3.

All capability stages are trained using supervised fine-tuning with the same optimization configuration unless otherwise specified. For the Pre-align architecture, the Temporal Encoder and alignment module are optimized in Stage 1, while the Temporal Encoder is frozen in subsequent stages and the remaining alignment components are updated. For the Post-align architecture, the Temporal Encoder and LLM backbone are jointly optimized. For the textualization setting, the numerical time series are directly converted into textual sequences and only the LLM is fine-tuned. Across different capability-development paths, we keep the model architecture, data split, initialization, and evaluation procedure unchanged so that performance differences mainly reflect the training stage and supervision strategy. This ensures a controlled comparison across different training paradigms.
For evaluation, Stage 1 separately measures categorical and numerical temporal perception, Stage 2 evaluates forecasting, imputation, anomaly detection, and classification, and Stage 3 evaluates high-level temporal reasoning. Forecasting and imputation use MSE, anomaly detection and classification use accuracy, while the reasoning dimensions are evaluated using multiple-choice accuracy when applicable. All reported comparative results are obtained under the same evaluation protocol within each experiment. This provides consistent evaluation criteria across all capability stages.
