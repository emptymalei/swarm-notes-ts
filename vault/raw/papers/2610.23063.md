Known Music, Unknown Response: Leakage-Audited Dynamic Arousal Forecasting With Scheduled Future Music
Bin Wen
Dai-Qiao Zhang
Tien-Ping Tan
Abstract
Dynamic music emotion recognition usually estimates concurrent or lag-aligned labels from audio that has already been observed. We instead forecast the change in consensus arousal when the upcoming music is scheduled but all later affect labels remain hidden. Evaluation holds out complete songs, encodes each eligible 4-s chunk independently, predicts arousal innovation, and compares the correct future with displaced segments from the same song. On PMEmo and a 1,494-song DEAM evaluation pool excluded from engineering development, a complete past/future MuQ representation raises concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) by 0.389–0.507 over recent affect and remains 0.268–0.382 above a calibrated nonlinear state model. A future-only trajectory contains no past or current audio and still exceeds that state model by 0.150–0.249. The earliest legal future chunk, which ends 5.5–11 s before the target, also remains informative. Correct placement exceeds an overlapping 5-s shift by 0.100–0.209 and the acoustic-nearest eligible same-song segment by 0.078–0.105, with positive song-cluster lower bounds. These results show that both scheduled content and its within-song position carry forecast information under the stated observation rules. The study provides a leakage-controlled reference for known-future music forecasting without treating predictive substitutions as causal interventions.

Introduction
Music emotion recognition (MER) estimates emotion expressed or perceived in music, frequently in Russell’s continuous valence–arousal space
[
1
]
. Dynamic MER extends this objective to a time-varying trajectory. Most systems nevertheless remain recognition models: the acoustic context around an annotated moment is observed, and the model estimates a concurrent or lag-adjusted label
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. This article asks a conditional forecasting question instead. If music after a decision time is already scheduled, but its later affective response has not been observed, can the scheduled segment predict a subsequent change in arousal?
The distinction matters in playlist planning, soundtrack adaptation, tutoring media, and other systems where a candidate stimulus is known before its response. Forecasting methods routinely condition on calendar variables or planned interventions whose future values are available at prediction time
[
6
]
. Music can be treated as such a known future covariate. Doing so does not reveal the future response, but it changes what a model may observe: it may inspect the scheduled waveform while it must not inspect affect labels after the forecast origin.
A concrete use is offline candidate screening. A soundtrack editor could provide a measured group-level starting state and a fixed set of licensed 10–15 s transitions, then use predicted arousal changes to reduce the set requiring human review. This use does not require the model to choose or causally optimize the final track. It does require a population and annotation protocol compatible with the training data, an observable starting state, the same audio-support rules, uncertainty estimates, and human safety and preference checks.
This setting is easy to evaluate incorrectly. Continuous emotion annotations are strongly autocorrelated, so a model can obtain a plausible score by copying the current state. Overlapping windows create many correlated examples from the same song, enabling track or production-style memorization. A bidirectional audio encoder can also cross the declared decision boundary if a long waveform is encoded before features are sliced. Finally, showing that audio improves over state does not establish that the
correctly placed
future segment matters; another segment from the same song may carry nearly the same global style and affect.

It formulates dynamic arousal forecasting with scheduled music as a known-future-covariate problem with explicit rules for outcomes, audio support, song splits, and temporal controls.
2.
It provides an information hierarchy that distinguishes recent affect, observed-past audio, future-only audio, and a complete past/future representation.
3.
It introduces state-preserving same-song controls that retain locality or acoustic similarity and therefore test within-song placement rather than merely track identity.

Dynamic Music Emotion Recognition

These systems and the present work solve related but nonidentical tasks. Standard dynamic MER observes acoustic evidence for the moment being labeled and often corrects the response lag. Our setting declares a forecast origin, hides all later annotations, and permits only music that is scheduled and known at that origin. Consequently, published dynamic-MER scores are not direct baselines: their targets, context, splits, and permitted information differ. We compare state and audio forecasts under the same observation rules and use song-disjoint validation.

2.2
Known Future Covariates and Innovation Targets
Multi-horizon forecasters often separate static, observed, and known-future variables
[
6
]
. A calendar event, tariff plan, or scheduled control may be known even when the corresponding outcome is not. Treating music as a scheduled covariate places affect forecasting within this framework. Predicting the innovation rather than the future level reduces the direct reward for persistence and makes the target closer to the transition a planning system would need to estimate. It does not remove all state predictability, so state-only models remain necessary controls.
Continuous emotion labels also contain annotation delay and smoothing. Explicit lag correction and joint alignment improve affect modeling
[
12
,
13
]
. We therefore insert a response delay between the permitted music support and the target, with a primary boundary gap of 2 s. Unlike label realignment, this boundary determines which audio chunks are legally observable by the forecasting model.
2.3
Music Foundation Representations and Interpretable Factors
MERT and MuQ learn transferable representations from large music collections
[
14
,
7
]
. Unified and multimodal MER research combines such embeddings with categorical, dimensional, lyrical, or musical features
[
15
,
16
]
. Representation quality alone, however, does not show that the correct within-song future is being used. Our negative controls hold the embedding family and song identity fixed while changing temporal placement.
Acoustic studies link loudness, timbre, rhythm, and tonality to multidimensional emotion ratings
[
17
]
. Music psychology further associates activation with dynamics, onset strength, temporal regularity, and tempo, whereas valence depends more strongly on harmony, context, familiarity, and semantic appraisal
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Predictive-processing accounts describe listening as hierarchical expectation formation, with sensory and cognitive expectations contributing separately to musical pleasure and surprise
[
21
,
22
]
. These findings motivate studying arousal, while they do not establish the incremental predictive value of a particular hand-crafted factor set.
2.4
Residual Learning, Cross-Fitting, and Negative Controls
Residual learning is a standard means of decomposing a forecasting problem, and recent neural objectives explicitly model remaining error streams
[
23
,
24
]
. Our audio head is fitted to out-of-fold state errors so that its training target does not use an in-sample state prediction. This uses sample separation, as in cross-fitting
[
25
,
26
]
, without introducing a Neyman-orthogonal score.
Negative controls are designed to reveal bias or an unintended pathway by preserving much of the data-generating context while disrupting the relation of interest
[
27
]
. A random different-song future is too easy for our purpose. We use same-song substitutions and quantify their temporal overlap or acoustic similarity. They are predictive controls, not counterfactual listener outcomes.

3.1.2
Four Evaluation Rules
The evaluation imposes four rules.
Hidden outcomes
: for a held-out prediction, no annotation after its forecast origin enters its input features. Outcomes from outer held-out songs are used only for evaluation, not for fitting, calibration, or model selection. Supervised fitting and inner validation use outcomes only from the corresponding training and development songs.
Separated audio support
: a frozen music encoder processes each 4-s chunk independently. A chunk is eligible only if its start and end lie entirely inside the declared observed-past or scheduled-future interval. Encoding a whole song and slicing hidden states afterward is prohibited because the encoder could have attended across the boundary.

State-Preserving Temporal Controls
The unrestricted control chooses an eligible anchor

A wrong-time segment can therefore remain better than state only; the relevant test is whether the correctly placed segment is better.

4.4
The Audio Representation Is Most Useful for Large Changes
CCC measures agreement over the complete innovation distribution, but a practical interpretation also requires knowing where absolute-error reductions occur. Figure
3
divides each dataset/horizon cell into quartiles of absolute ground-truth innovation, with cut points computed within that cell. The state model has the lowest MAE in the smallest-change quartile, where predicting almost no change is an effective strategy. This explains why persistence is a strong local default even when its pooled CCC is low.
The ordering reverses for the largest changes. In every cell, the correctly placed audio representation has lower Q4 MAE than both state and wrong-time audio. For example, DEAM Q4 MAE falls from 0.139 to 0.104 at 10 s and from 0.186 to 0.124 at 15 s; the wrong-time alternatives reach only 0.122 and 0.151. PMEmo shows the same pattern. Thus, scheduled audio earns its aggregate advantage by forecasting substantial departures from inertia, while the near-zero regime remains a boundary where a conservative state prediction is preferable. This analysis was performed on the fixed OOF predictions and does not alter training or selection.

4.5
Innovation-Label Reliability
Finally, split-half rater agreement provides scale for the innovation target. Randomly splitting DEAM raters and recomputing two independent group-average arousal trajectories gives CCC 0.472 at 10 s and 0.529 at 15 s over 200 splits (95% intervals [0.450, 0.493] and [0.504, 0.553]). The complete-representation scores of 0.458/0.564 are in the same range. This comparison is not a human-performance ceiling: each split-half trajectory averages only about half of the raters, whereas the prediction target averages all available raters and is therefore less noisy. A model trained across songs can estimate systematic structure in that all-rater mean and can slightly exceed half-versus-half agreement. This comparison documents disagreement between half panels; it neither bounds attainable performance on the all-rater target nor quantifies the remaining room for improvement.
5
Discussion
5.1
Scheduled-Audio Forecast Information
The 0.389–0.507 CCC lift is the value of the complete scheduled-audio representation over a recent-affect ridge. The future-only analysis shows that much of this information is present in the candidate segment itself: it remains 0.150–0.249 above a nonlinear state model and 0.151–0.261 above past-only audio. This is the primary result.
5.2
Content Versus Placement
Song-disjoint downstream fitting keeps held-out songs out of forecasting-head training. Overlap between these songs and the foundation encoders’ pretraining corpora has not been excluded. This split also does not remove global musical structure. A wrong-time segment still carries style, production, instrumentation, tempo range, and broad affect. This explains why the complete action lift is larger than the within-song placement margin. The overlapping-shift and acoustic-nearest controls ask a narrower question: whether the correct location remains informative after much of the track-level and local acoustic content is preserved. Their positive margins support temporal specificity, but the residual value of wrong-time audio also shows that the representation uses both track-level content and local placement.
These substitutions are not listener counterfactuals. They test the sensitivity of a fitted forecast while state and song identity are held fixed. A causal music-selection policy would require randomized or logged actions, listener-level outcomes, and assumptions beyond those used here.
5.3
Relation to Dynamic MER
Numerical rank claims against ordinary dynamic MER would be misleading. Recognition systems often observe acoustic context at or around the labeled moment, may align labels retrospectively, and may use different song or window splits. Our models expose scheduled audio at a declared origin and score a later innovation. The appropriate comparisons are therefore models trained under the same observation rules. Within that setting, future-only audio remains useful without past descriptors, the complete representation adds further relational structure, and the same-song controls verify temporal specificity. These results provide a reference for the forecasting task, not a replacement for recognition benchmarks.
The same evaluation logic can apply when a future video scene, dialogue response, lesson, or notification is scheduled before its user response is observed: declare the origin, isolate permitted feature support, hold out contextual units, predict change, and test temporal placement with within-context substitutions.
5.4
Use Case and Limitations
One immediate use is offline pre-screening rather than automated intervention. Given an observed group-level state and a fixed set of candidate transitions, a media editor could rank or filter segments for human review. The model would estimate expected arousal change; it would not decide which segment causes a desired response. Practical use would additionally require calibrated uncertainty, population matching, listener or group covariates, preference and safety constraints, and prospective validation. Consensus labels cannot represent stable subgroups of listeners who respond in opposite ways.
The study is limited to arousal; the retained analyses do not establish
corresponding results for valence. The models use frozen MuQ representations
and simple regression heads. They do not establish whether a large
fine-tuned sequence model would improve the same forecasting task.
An audit of the earlier explicit-factor experiments found that their
acoustic extraction support could cross the declared observation boundaries.
We therefore exclude those factor predictors and their augmentation,
capacity, backbone, and response-delay results from the evidence reported
in this preprint. They require support-correct feature extraction and
new evaluation before they can support a scientific claim. The retained
predictors use affect state and independently encoded MuQ chunks, without
an explicit-factor correction.
PMEmo and DEAM do not establish cross-cultural, artist-disjoint, or listener-specific transport. The common-song PMEmo analysis removes the immediate 10-s/15-s composition difference, but the horizons still represent different prediction targets and should not be interpreted as a pure response curve. Future work should retain synchronized per-rater trajectories and metadata, group by artist or release, use a denser set of horizons, and evaluate controlled resynthesis that changes one musical factor while preserving others.
Finally, window counts are not independent sample sizes. Song-disjoint fitting and song-cluster resampling address the main dependence, but the reported intervals remain conditional on the fixed representation and development path. They do not replace independent replication.
5.5
Threats to Validity
Construct validity
: the target is consensus perceived/expressed arousal innovation, not an individual’s felt emotion. Split-half agreement indicates label noise, but does not resolve the construct gap.
Internal validity
: outcome and encoder leakage are addressed through declared support and nesting. Nevertheless, representations can reflect unmeasured production variables, and observational same-song controls cannot establish intervention effects.
External validity
: results cover PMEmo and an engineering-excluded DEAM evaluation pool at 10 and 15 s. They do not establish performance for other genres, cultures, annotation protocols, or longer policies.
Statistical conclusion validity
: windows are highly correlated. All splits and bootstrap units are songs, and repeat/fold dispersion is inspected separately. Diagnostic analyses are not relabeled as confirmatory evidence.
6
Conclusion
Music may be known before its response is known. This article formalized that distinction as dynamic arousal forecasting with a scheduled future covariate and evaluated it under explicit rules for outcomes, audio support, song splits, and temporal controls. A complete independently encoded MuQ representation improved CCC by 0.389–0.507 over recent affect and remained 0.268–0.382 above a calibrated nonlinear state model, while future-only audio remained well above both that model and a matched past-only trajectory. The first legal 4-s chunk remained informative 5.5–11 s before the target, and correct placement survived strongly overlapping shifts and acoustic-nearest same-song controls. These results show that both scheduled content and its within-song timing contain forecast information.
The contribution is a known-future forecasting framework in which state, future content, and temporal placement are evaluated separately. These results concern consensus arousal under the tested corpora and observation rules, and do not establish causal music-selection effects or generalization to individual listeners.
