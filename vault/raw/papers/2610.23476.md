Abstract
Change points that arrive in quick succession, or near the boundaries of the sample, are prone to type-II error: the few observations trapped between successive breaks carry too little information for global procedures to analyze. This article develops a test statistic,
I
1
I_{1}
, for which we derive the limiting null distribution under Gaussian noise, extend the theory to weakly dependent sequences, and prove consistency under a mean-shift alternative. Around
I
1
I_{1}
, we construct DCI, a two-phase algorithm that couples a global scan with multiple local tests via an adaptive-width sliding window. In extensive simulations,
I
1
I_{1}
and DCI remain Pareto-optimal over criteria spaces spanning true and false positive rates, localization error, and segmentation covering. An application to the US-Iran 2026 conflict quantifies the exposure of energy markets to geopolitical shocks using two new metrics, event sensitivity and event attribution, leading to time series tokenization.

Introduction
Change point detection, the identification of time points at which the statistical properties of a series shift abruptly, is a central problem in time series analysis. In finance, economics, engineering, and environmental studies alike, a reliable account of structural shifts is a prerequisite for explainable modeling, accurate forecasting, and sound decision-making, whether the object of interest is a market regime change or the impact of a macroeconomic policy. Modern data environments, however, increasingly confront the analyst with series in which change points occur in quick succession and separated by too few observations. Volatile, high-frequency financial data during geopolitical crises are a leading example. Conventional detection methods, designed with well-separated breaks in mind, often struggle in precisely these settings, and this difficulty motivates the present article.
The literature on change point analysis is vast, and we can only outline the strands most relevant to our work. A classical formulation casts change detection as a model selection problem
(
Truong et al., 2020
)
, resolved through likelihood ratio maximization, with foundational contributions by
Hinkley (1970)
and multivariate mean change tests developed by
Srivastava and Worsley (1986)
. In parallel, nonparametric statistics, most prominently the CUSUM of
Page (1954)
and its extensions
(
Fremdt, 2015
;
Han et al., 2023
;
Inclan and Tiao, 1994
)
, dispense with distributional assumptions and offer model-free alternatives. Within this tradition, solutions are now available for changes in the mean
(
Pettitt, 1979
)
, high-dimensional means
(
Drikvandi and Modarres, 2025
)
, the slope
(
Fearnhead et al., 2019
)
, the variance
(
Qin et al., 2017
)
, and the covariance structure
(
Londschien et al., 2021
)
. Further nonparametric approaches include rank- and U-statistic-based procedures for high-dimensional data
(
Lévy-Leduc and Roueff, 2009
)
, graph-based methods
(
Chen and Zhang, 2015
)
, and multiple change-point detection in multivariate data
(
Matteson and James, 2014
)
. For the multiple change point problem specifically, exact search procedures based on dynamic programming trace back to the segment neighborhood algorithm of
Auger and Lawrence (1989)
, with computationally efficient descendants in the pruned exact linear time (PELT) method
(
Killick et al., 2012
)
, CPOP
(
Fearnhead et al., 2019
)
, and RFPOP
(
Fearnhead and Rigaill, 2019
)
. Nonparametric maximum-likelihood formulations of the multiple change problem typically select the number of breaks by minimizing a penalty function
(
Zou et al., 2014
)
. A second prominent family builds on binary segmentation, a ‘greedy’ strategy that ignores the global configuration at each split; refinements include circular binary segmentation
(
Olshen et al., 2004
)
, wild binary segmentation
(
Fryzlewicz, 2014
)
, sparsified binary segmentation
(
Cho and Fryzlewicz, 2015
)
, and seeded binary segmentation
(
Kovács et al., 2023
)
, whereas ‘generous’ bottom-up alternatives are reviewed by
Truong et al. (2020)
. Additional proposals of practical importance include TrendSegment
(
Maeng and Fryzlewicz, 2024
)
, the change point procedure via pruned objectives
(
Zhang et al., 2017
)
, and the narrowest-over-threshold method
(
Baranowski et al., 2019b
)
, which blends nonparametric and parametric elements sequentially. The MOSUM procedure of
Eichinger and Kirch (2018)
detects multiple changes in the mean by scanning standardized differences between sample means computed over adjacent moving windows and identifying threshold-exceeding local maxima as change point estimates. Although the proposed
I
1
I_{1}
and DCI also deploy a sliding window, they differ from MOSUM in both design and performance. Comparison of the proposed and existing methods (Section
3
) shows that the proposals outperform the MOSUM procedure across a range of scenarios. Finally, kernel-based procedures
(
Arlot et al., 2019
)
and supervised learning methods, largely classification-based and driven by sensor data for activity recognition, are surveyed by

. Common to nearly all of these proposals, however, is the implicit assumption that successive change points are separated by segments long enough to permit reliable estimation on either side.
Three considerations distinguish our contribution from this literature. First, methods for multiple change points typically privilege a global view of the series; this is necessary for comprehensive detection, but might compromise localized insight when breaks arrive in quick succession due to the near-insufficient number of observations between successive change points, inflating type-II error. Second, the computational burden of existing procedures grows rapidly with the number of candidate breaks and the forms of change, limiting scalability. Third, breaks near the extremities of the sample remain notoriously difficult to detect. We address all three issues by proposing a new test statistic,
I
1
I_{1}
, whose asymptotic properties and limiting null distribution we establish, and a two-phase algorithm, the Detector of Change-points using
I
1
I_{1}
(DCI), which reconciles a global scan with multiple local tests. A new design feature, an adaptive-width sliding window, enables
I
1
I_{1}
and DCI to retain high true detection rates under the afore-stated irregular configurations.

Conclusion
In this article, we develop a new test statistic,
I
1
I_{1}
, for detecting a level change in time series, establishing its asymptotic properties and limiting null distribution under i.i.d. Gaussian and weakly dependent settings, and proving consistency under a mean-shift alternative. Building on
I

I_{1}
, we propose DCI, a two-phase algorithm for the closely-spaced multiple change points regime that reconciles a global scan with local tests through an adaptive-width sliding window. Simulations show that
I

I_{1}
and DCI remain Pareto-optimal over the TPR-FPR-MAE (Section
3.1
) and TPR-FPT-MAE-USCM (Section
3.2
) criteria spaces, respectively, with pronounced advantages when breaks occur near sample extremities or in rapid succession. An application to the US-Iran 2026 conflict shows that the financial instruments cluster into a coherent pattern of exposure across energy markets, and that the ES and EA features devised in Section
4
constitute an information-rich, reduced-dimensional set of variables for time series analysis. Future research will proceed in two directions. First, we will extend
I
1
I_{1}
to detect changes in trend and in variance, yielding
I
2
I_{2}
and
I
3
I_{3}
. Second, we will pursue time series tokenization: the embedding of a series of
T

observations into low-dimensional ES-EA pairs computed against global disruption event sequences, such as wars or Artificial Intelligence model launches. In view of the growing frequency of such disruptions, this tokenization offers a compact and portable representation for assessing their impact on commodities, technology sector indices, or indeed any real-valued temporal sequence of interest.
