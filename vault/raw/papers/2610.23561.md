Abstract
Climate emulators often enforce physical constraints by correcting each state predicted by a neural network, but evaluating only the corrected forecast conflates predictor skill with the effect of the correction. We introduce an evaluation framework that separates these contributions by comparing corrected forecasts, pre-correction predictions along corrected trajectories, and autonomous rollouts with correction disabled. We further cross correction during training and inference to distinguish its immediate effect from how it shapes the learned predictor, and use matched nonphysical controls to test whether improvements are specific to the physical correction. In ACE2, correction substantially improves physical consistency. In a three-seed ACE2 pilot, models trained with correction produce less accurate uncorrected precipitation forecasts at short horizons than models trained without correction, but more accurate forecasts at 10 and 30 days. This contradicts our preregistered hypothesis that the deficit would grow with forecast horizon. In a separate nine-seed experiment, directly supervising the uncorrected output reverses its precipitation forecast deficit at 72 hours while maintaining the accuracy of corrected forecasts at 24 hours. In ConCerNet’s heat-equation system, projection-trained models have higher uncorrected trajectory-based vector-field RMSE than models trained without projection, while restoring the physical projection recovers accuracy far more effectively than a matched nonphysical control. Together, these results show that hard corrections can reshape the predictors trained alongside them, creating dependencies that are not visible from corrected forecasts alone. Hard-constrained emulators should therefore be evaluated by separately measuring predictor accuracy, correction effects, and their interaction during training.

Introduction
Learned weather and climate models increasingly combine a neural predictor with a deterministic operation that enforces conservation, positivity, diagnostic closure, or prescribed boundary conditions
(
Beucler et al., 2021
;
Watt-Meyer and others, 2025
)
. These operations can improve a forecast while concealing errors in the state predicted by the network
(
Chapman et al., 2026
)
. Evaluation of the corrected output alone cannot distinguish an accurate learned transition from an inaccurate transition whose errors are subsequently removed.
Training may improve the predictor itself; the corrector may repair a largely unchanged prediction; or the predictor may adapt to a correction available at every step
(
Um et al., 2020
;
Chapman et al., 2026
)
.
Our proposed evaluation uses three complementary comparisons. Matched controls test whether the physical correction contributes more than an equal computation budget or a nonphysical perturbation. We then evaluate network outputs immediately before correction along corrected trajectories. Finally, we run models trained with and without correction in both inference modes, keeping each model’s learned weights fixed. Disabling correction throughout forecasting makes the predictor repeatedly receive its own uncorrected outputs, exposing the feedback-dependent input distributions studied in sequential prediction
(
Ross et al., 2011
)
. Figure

outlines evaluation (a,b), findings (c), and raw-output supervision (d).

ACE2
(

provides a revealing case study. Its corrector greatly improves conservation and positivity, but the precipitation advantage depends on forecast horizon. With correction disabled throughout forecasting, models trained with correction have higher precipitation RMSE than models trained without it at 24 and 72 h, but lower RMSE at 10 and 30 days. (

We evaluate it through the ACE2 case study, a nine-seed ConCerNet experiment in a separate constrained dynamical system, and within-family comparisons of total-energy correction in ACE2S-SHiELD+.

3.2
Matched-Control Evaluation in ACE2
We compare the official physical correction with four controls: no correction, longitude shift, latitude-band permutation, and spectrum-preserving phase randomization. All models receive 2400 adaptation updates, with the common update budget selected using validation data. Each model uses the same correction condition during adaptation and forecasting, so these comparisons include both adaptation and inference effects.
Evaluation uses nine seeds, four months from 1940, forecast horizons of 24 and 72 hours, and three variables: near-surface temperature, 500 hPa height, and precipitation. The evaluation months are held out from local adaptation but fall within the released model’s pretraining period
(
Watt-Meyer and others, 2025
)
.
The three spatial shams disrupt the spatial alignment of the correction. Spatial phase randomization follows the surrogate-data principle of preserving spectral properties while disrupting other structure
(
Theiler et al., 1992
)
. We evaluate predictive accuracy together with conservation, positivity, correction magnitude, and decorrelation.

3.5
Evaluation in a Heat-Equation System
We apply the crossed training–inference design to ConCerNet’s 101-dimensional heat-equation system
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. Our experiment uses a known analytic invariant in place of learning one. The network predicts a vector field, and physical projection subtracts the mean of its components from each component. The projected vector field therefore sums to zero, enforcing conservation of the sum of state components at the vector-field level.
Networks trained with and without projection start from identical weights and use the same training data and minibatch order. Each network is evaluated with and without physical projection. An additional sham projection is matched in norm and constructed independently of evaluation targets.
The prediction metric is trajectory-based vector-field RMSE. At a forecast horizon of one time unit, we evaluate the model’s vector field at the forecast state and the reference vector field at the reference state, then compute their RMSE. This metric combines differences in the vector fields and their evaluation states; state RMSE is a separate quantity.

4.3
Horizon-Dependent Reversal of the Uncorrected Gap
With correction disabled throughout forecasting, predictors adapted with correction have higher precipitation RMSE at short horizons and lower RMSE at longer horizons than predictors adapted without correction (Figure
2
; Table
4
). Mean seed-level relative increases are 59.7% at 24 hours and 7.13% at 72 hours; the 72-hour increases range from 7.04% to 7.17% across the three seeds. At 10 and 30 days, the comparison reverses in all three seeds, with mean relative RMSE reductions of 13.48% and 15.69%. This reversal contradicts our preregistered hypothesis that the uncorrected deficit grows with forecast horizon.
The pattern also varies across variables. At 24 hours, adaptation with correction increases uncorrected 500 hPa height RMSE by 93.72% on average, while enabling inference correction for that predictor reduces its height RMSE by 51.58%. At 10 days, the 13.48% reduction in uncorrected precipitation RMSE contrasts with increases of 11.51% in temperature RMSE and 9.17% in height RMSE. The improvement at 10 days is therefore specific to precipitation among the three evaluated variables.

4.6

5
Discussion
In the ACE2 pilot, the effect of correction varies with adaptation history and forecast horizon. At longer horizons, the predictor adapted with correction has lower uncorrected precipitation RMSE than the predictor adapted without it, yet enabling its corrector increases RMSE. For the predictor adapted without correction, enabling correction reduces mean precipitation RMSE at every evaluated horizon. These results motivate evaluating coupled trajectories, as in prognostic parameterization tests
(
Brenowitz and Bretherton, 2018
)
and solver-in-the-loop learning
(
Um et al., 2020
)
.
The variable-specific patterns in Table
4
suggest useful diagnostics. Following ACE2’s separate assessments of bias and variability
(
Watt-Meyer and others, 2025
)
, we propose examining precipitation bias, variability, and spatial agreement alongside errors in other fields. Tracking correction magnitudes and water-budget residuals over forecast lead could help assess whether changes in the applied correction coincide with the reversal.
The direct-supervision experiment links the short-horizon deficit to the adaptation objective: supervising pre-correction precipitation reverses the 72-hour uncorrected RMSE gap while also improving corrected 24-hour accuracy (Table
2
). This complements the raw-amplitude stabilization studied by
Chapman et al. (2026)
by evaluating the intervention under recursive forecasting.
ConCerNet extends predictor-corrector dependence to a constrained dynamical system with an analytic invariant
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. Physical projection recovers prediction accuracy that a norm-matched sham does not (Figure
4
). Together, the ACE2 and ConCerNet results demonstrate the value of evaluating predictor accuracy and correction effects separately, including under crossed training–inference conditions.
6
Limitations
The climate experiments use adapted ACE2-family checkpoints with prescribed sea-surface temperature and sea ice
(
Watt-Meyer and others, 2025
)
. Their 1940 evaluation months fall within checkpoint pretraining, leaving generalization to unseen periods untested. The horizon-dependent reversal is based on three seeds, while the longer-horizon matched-regime comparisons remain descriptive. Direct supervision was evaluated at 72 hours without correction and 24 hours with correction. Extending these findings beyond the ACE2 family and the heat-equation ConCerNet system to other models and correction schemes remains an open direction.
7
Conclusion
Separating corrected forecasts, pre-correction outputs, and uncorrected rollouts reveals how physical correction interacts with the learned predictor. Across the tested ACE2 and ConCerNet settings, this interaction depends on training conditions, forecast horizon, and system. Direct supervision improves the short-horizon ACE2 predictor while preserving corrected accuracy. Evaluating these components separately therefore provides a clearer account of forecast skill in models that use physical correction.

Appendix B
Experimental and Statistical Details
B.1
Direct Supervision of Uncorrected Outputs
Equation
6
defines the auxiliary objective; its normalization and validation-only weight selection are described in the main text. Here we give the prespecified initiation rule and held-out evaluation design.
The preregistered joint hypothesis requires a 72-hour uncorrected precipitation deficit in all three pilot seeds, growth of the deficit across horizons, and a mean 24-hour precipitation RMSE reduction of at least 0.5% when correction is restored. The direct-supervision experiment is initiated when the first and third criteria hold.
The direct-supervision evaluation uses nine adaptation seeds (7, 11, 23, 31, 43, 59, 71, 89, and 101) and two evaluation months, June and September 1940. Forecasts are evaluated at 24, 72, 240, and 720 hours. The supervised model is run with and without correction and compared with three matched baselines: the correction-trained model with correction enabled or disabled, and the model trained and evaluated without correction. The reported intervention criteria use the 72-hour uncorrected precipitation gap and the 24-hour corrected precipitation RMSE change.
B.2
Statistical Analysis
To account for training randomness
(
Bouthillier et al., 2021
)
, training seed is the independent unit. Months and forecast starts are paired observations nested within seed.
For the matched-control experiment, we use bootstrap resampling
(

)
to report seed-level effects and pointwise 95% hierarchical percentile-bootstrap intervals, using 10,000 resamples of seeds, nested months, and paired starts. Exact one-sided sign-flip tests
(

)

(
Holm, 1979
)
across the prespecified family of 24 comparisons. Long-horizon analyses are descriptive secondary outcomes.
Practical importance is assessed by comparing each unrounded mean relative RMSE improvement with a threshold of 0.5% for temperature and 5% for height and precipitation. The threshold classification is based on the mean effect; bootstrap intervals describe uncertainty around that estimate.
For the three-seed training–inference pilot, Figure

B.3
Physical-Consistency Pilot
The physical-consistency comparison uses seeds 7, 11, and 23 and seven forecast starts, 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21, and 25 October 1940, with 72-hour rollouts. For each seed, dry-air drift is the maximum absolute change in area-weighted global-mean dry-air surface pressure over forecast starts and saved leads, relative to initialization. We then average these maxima across seeds. Water-budget residuals are area-weighted absolute means, averaged over starts, saved leads, and seeds. The negative-precipitation fraction is area-weighted and averaged over starts, the 24- and 72-hour endpoints, and seeds.
